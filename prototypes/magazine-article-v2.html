The first thing you notice is the quiet. After the airport, the ferry terminal and the taxi with the radio all the way up, Paros at seven in the morning feels like someone finally turned the volume down.

We came for a week, eight of us on a forty-two-foot catamaran, with a skipper who has been sailing these waters since he was tall enough to see over the wheel. The plan was loose by design. Sail three or four hours a day, anchor somewhere sheltered, swim, eat, repeat. Nikos decided the route each morning based on the wind, which in the Cyclades in July means the meltemi, the strong northerly that can turn a pleasant crossing into a lively one.

By the second day, nobody was checking their phone. By the third, nobody knew what day it was. By the fourth, a debate broke out over whether we should simply never go home.

Life on board.

A catamaran is, in the most practical sense, a floating apartment with two front porches.

The cabins are compact but private, each with its own small bathroom, and the deck is wide enough for eight people to sprawl without anyone touching. The net at the front became the most fought-over spot on the boat, especially at sunset.

Mornings started early. Someone would put the coffee on, someone else would jump straight off the back step into water so clear you could count the stones on the bottom. Lunch was usually at anchor: tomatoes that tasted like tomatoes, feta, bread from whatever bakery we'd found the night before.
