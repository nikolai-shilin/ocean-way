How a yacht steers under engine

A car steers with its front wheels and stops with its brakes. A sailing yacht has neither. Under engine she steers with the rudder, which only works while water flows past it, and she stops only by going astern. Most charter yachts have one propeller just in front of the rudder, on a shaft or on a saildrive (a leg that goes down through the bottom of the hull).

Gear and throttle are usually one lever. Pushed forward from upright, it engages ahead; pulled back, astern. Further in either direction adds revs. Always change direction at idle and pause in neutral for a second or two. Slamming from ahead to astern at high revs damages the gearbox.

At walking pace the rudder has little grip, so the boat answers slowly and the wind pushes her around. Speed gives control, but also momentum. The skill is to keep just enough speed, and to use short bursts of power when you need more grip.

The pivot point

Going ahead, a yacht turns around a point roughly a third of her length back from the bow, near the mast. The bow moves into the turn and the stern swings out the other way, often wider than you expect. So when you turn away from a pontoon or another boat, the stern swings towards it.

Going astern, the pivot point moves aft, towards the stern. Now the bow swings wide, and the wind catches it easily.

Tip

Before a turn in a tight marina, look behind you as well as ahead. Ask a crew member to watch the stern and call out the distance.

Prop wash: steering without speed

The propeller drives a stream of water, the prop wash, straight back over the rudder. Going ahead, this wash makes the rudder work even when the boat is hardly moving. Put the wheel hard over and give a short, firm burst ahead: the stern kicks sideways before the boat gains much speed. Then back to neutral.

Going astern, the wash flows forward, away from the rudder, so it does not help you steer. Astern you steer only with the boat's own speed through the water.

Prop walk

A turning propeller also pushes the stern sideways, a little going ahead and much more going astern. This is prop walk. Most charter yachts have a right-handed propeller: seen from behind, it turns clockwise going ahead. Going astern, it kicks the stern to port.

Prop walk is strongest when you first go astern from a standstill with plenty of revs. Once the boat is moving astern and water flows past the rudder, you can steer against it. Saildrives usually walk less than shafts, and every boat is different, so test yours on the first day. In open water, stop, go astern firmly and watch which way the stern goes, and how far.

Then use it. Coming alongside port side to, a burst astern to stop brings the stern in towards the quay. Starboard side to, it pulls the stern away, so come in slower and at a shallower angle.

Going astern

  • Face aft. Stand behind the wheel facing the stern and steer as if the stern were the bow: turn the wheel the way you want the stern to go.
  • Get moving first. Start with a firm burst to overcome prop walk, then reduce revs once she is moving and answering the wheel.
  • Hold the wheel. Going astern, the water pushes hard on the rudder and can spin the wheel out of your hands. Keep a firm grip and make small corrections.
  • Keep it slow. For mooring stern-to you want walking pace or less. If she swings off line, go to neutral, give a short burst ahead with the wheel turned to push the stern back, then carry on astern.

Stopping, and the wind

A 12-metre yacht weighs about 8 to 10 tonnes and keeps moving long after you take the power off. Plan to arrive at walking pace and stop with a short burst astern. Remember that the burst also brings prop walk.

At low speed the wind has more effect than the engine. The bow is light and has no keel under it, so it blows away downwind first while the keel holds the middle of the boat. Going astern, the stern tends to turn towards the wind. Use a little more speed in a strong breeze, and plan each manoeuvre so the wind helps you rather than fights you.

If an approach goes wrong, give up early: go back to open water and try again. Nobody minds a second attempt; everyone minds a damaged boat.

Turning in a tight space

To turn a yacht around in a narrow marina lane, combine prop wash and prop walk. With a right-handed propeller, turn to starboard (clockwise), because then prop walk helps:

  1. Start in the middle of the lane, almost stopped.
  2. Put the wheel hard to starboard and leave it there for the whole turn.
  3. Give a short, firm burst ahead. The prop wash kicks the stern to port and the bow turns to starboard. Go to neutral before the boat gains much speed.
  4. Give a short, firm burst astern. Prop walk kicks the stern to port again and stops the boat moving forward.
  5. Repeat ahead and astern until she points the way you want, then straighten the wheel and motor away.

Turning to port works too, but it is harder, because prop walk then works against you astern. If the boat will not come round, check the wind: the bursts need to be firmer when it blows on the bow.

Watch out

Before you engage gear near a quay or a mooring buoy, check that no line is in the water. Slack mooring lines, a slipped spring and the dinghy painter can all wrap round a propeller in seconds.

Practise it on the water

On our courses you practise all of this in open water before you try it in a harbour: stopping next to a buoy, holding the boat still against the wind, going astern in a straight line and turning in the boat's own length. Each crew member helms until the boat does what they expect, and then you take her into the marina.
