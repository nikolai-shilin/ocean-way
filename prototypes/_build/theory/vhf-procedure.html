Channel 16 and working channels

A marine VHF radio works by line of sight, so its range is usually 20–30 miles between ships and further to a coast station with a tall aerial. It has many channels, but one matters most.

Channel 16 is for distress, urgency and safety calls, and for calling another station. Every vessel with a VHF should listen to it at sea. You use it to make contact, then move to a working channel to talk.

  • Working channels. Ship-to-ship chat goes on intership channels such as 6, 8, 72 or 77. Marinas and ports each have their own channel, listed in the pilot book.
  • Channel 70 carries digital DSC alerts only. You never speak on it.
  • Dual watch. This setting lets the radio listen to channel 16 and one other channel at the same time. It is useful in a marina approach, but keep 16 in the loop.
  • Power. Use low power (1 watt) for nearby stations such as a marina, and high power (25 watts) for distance.

The DSC distress button

Most modern sets have DSC (Digital Selective Calling). Each boat has an MMSI (Maritime Mobile Service Identity), a nine-digit number programmed into the radio. When the radio is connected to the GPS, it knows the boat's position.

To send a distress alert, lift the cover over the red DISTRESS button and hold it down (usually for about five seconds) until the radio transmits. If there is time, first choose the nature of distress from the menu, such as fire or sinking. The alert carries your MMSI, position and time.

What happens next:

  1. The alert goes out on channel 70 and the radio switches itself to channel 16.
  2. The radio repeats the alert every few minutes until a station acknowledges it.
  3. A coast station or rescue centre acknowledges by DSC.
  4. You then make the Mayday voice call on channel 16 and keep listening.

Mayday

Mayday is for grave and imminent danger to a vessel or a person, when you need immediate help: fire out of control, sinking, a person overboard you cannot recover. Speak slowly and clearly on channel 16, high power. The format:

  1. MAYDAY, MAYDAY, MAYDAY
  2. THIS IS, followed by the boat name three times
  3. Call sign and MMSI
  4. MAYDAY, boat name
  5. Position
  6. Nature of distress
  7. Assistance required
  8. Number of persons on board
  9. Any other useful information
  10. OVER

A full example:

"Mayday, Mayday, Mayday. This is sailing yacht Kalimera, Kalimera, Kalimera. Call sign Sierra Victor Alfa Two, MMSI two three nine one two three four five six. Mayday Kalimera. My position two miles south of Cape Sounion. Fire in the engine compartment, out of control. I require immediate assistance. Six persons on board. We are preparing to abandon to the liferaft. Over."

Then wait. If nobody answers, repeat the call. Give position as latitude and longitude from the GPS, or as a bearing and distance from a charted point.

Pan-Pan

Pan-Pan is the urgency call. Use it when the safety of the boat or a person is at risk, but the danger is not immediate: engine failure near a lee shore with time in hand, a serious injury, a broken rudder. The structure follows the Mayday, addressed to all stations.

"Pan-Pan, Pan-Pan, Pan-Pan. All stations, all stations, all stations. This is sailing yacht Kalimera, Kalimera, Kalimera. Call sign Sierra Victor Alfa Two, MMSI two three nine one two three four five six. My position two miles south of Kalymnos. Engine failure and torn mainsail, drifting slowly, no immediate danger. I require a tow. Four persons on board. Over."

Sécurité

Sécurité (say "say-cure-ee-tay") is the safety call, for navigation or weather warnings. Coast stations use it for gale warnings. You might use it to warn others of a floating container or an unlit buoy. Announce it on channel 16, "Sécurité, Sécurité, Sécurité, all stations, this is…", name the working channel, then switch there and give the message.

Calling a marina

Most of your radio use is ordinary. To call a marina, set low power and the marina's working channel:

"Kos Marina, Kos Marina, this is sailing yacht Kalimera, Kalimera. Over."

When they answer, say what you need: "Good afternoon. We are a 12-metre yacht, beam four metres, draft two metres, requesting a berth for two nights. We are 15 minutes away. Over." They will give you a berth and often ask you to wait outside for a marinero (berthing assistant). Prepare lines and fenders while you wait.

Procedure words

  • Over: I have finished; I expect a reply.
  • Out: the conversation is finished. Never say "over and out".
  • Say again: please repeat.
  • Received: I have your message.
  • This is: before your own name.
  • Correction: I made a mistake; here is the right version.
  • Seelonce Mayday: silence. Used by the station controlling a distress. Stop transmitting on that channel until you hear Seelonce Fini.

Licences

To operate a marine VHF with DSC you need an operator's certificate, normally the Short Range Certificate (SRC) or an equivalent. The boat also needs a ship station licence from its flag state, which lists its call sign and MMSI. Charter yachts are already licensed, but the operator's certificate is personal and is your responsibility.

Watch out

Never test-transmit a Mayday, by voice or by pressing the red button. A false alert can launch a real rescue. Use your radio's DSC test call instead. If you send a distress alert by mistake, do not switch off. Cancel the alert on the radio, then call on channel 16: "All stations, all stations, all stations, this is (boat name ×3), MMSI…, position…, cancel my distress alert of (time). Over."

Practise it on the water

On our courses you practise every call on training radios first, until the formats come without notes. On passage you then handle the real everyday calls yourself, calling marinas and other boats on the working channels.
