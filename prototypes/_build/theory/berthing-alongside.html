When you berth alongside

Berthing alongside means tying up with the side of the boat against a quay, a pontoon or another boat. In most Mediterranean harbours you moor stern-to instead, which has its own guide in this series. But you will still come alongside often:

  • Fuel docks. Almost always alongside, often in a tight, busy spot.
  • Town quays. In Turkey and Greece, where there is space, you can often lie alongside a quiet stretch of wall. Ask the harbourmaster if in doubt.
  • Pontoons. Waiting and reception pontoons in marinas, and visitors' pontoons in some harbours.
  • Rafting up. Alongside another boat when the quay is full.

The lines and their names

Names for springs vary between schools, so on board it is clearest to say where a line starts and which way it leads.

  • Bow line. From the bow (front), led forward to the shore. Holds the bow in.
  • Stern line. From the stern (back), led aft to the shore. Holds the stern in.
  • Fore spring. From the forward part of the boat, led aft to the shore. Stops her moving forward.
  • Aft spring. From the after part of the boat, led forward to the shore. Stops her moving back.
  • Breast lines. Short lines straight across to the shore. Fine for a short stop, but they snatch as the boat moves.

Together, these keep the boat still and parallel to the quay. Many charter yachts also have a midships cleat near the widest part of the boat. A line from it led aft to the shore, a midships spring, is the most useful first line when you come alongside.

Prepare before you go in

Set everything up in open water, and decide which side you will tie up on.

  • Fenders. At least three on the berthing side, along the widest part of the hull. Hang them where the hull will touch: low for a floating pontoon, higher for a stone quay or another yacht's topsides (the sides of the hull above the water). Keep a spare fender in a crew member's hands.
  • Bow and stern lines. Cleated on board, led outside the guardrails and stanchions, and coiled ready.
  • Springs. The midships spring ready first, then the fore and aft springs.
  • Crew briefed. Who steps off with which line, who takes in slack and who fends off. Agree hand signals, since the helm often cannot hear the deck.

Brief the crew to step off, never jump. Wait until the boat is close and almost stopped, then step from the widest part of the boat, near the shrouds (the wires holding the mast), line in hand. Nobody puts a hand or foot between boat and quay, or tries to stop a moving yacht by pushing.

The approach: angle, wind and prop walk

Approach slowly, with just enough speed to steer, at about 20 to 30 degrees to the quay. Aim a little ahead of where you want to stop. A few metres off, turn parallel and stop her alongside.

Wind onto the quay

Easy coming in, harder leaving. Approach at a shallower angle, stop parallel a metre or two off and let the wind push you in. Check the fenders, because she will land with some weight.

Wind off the quay

The bow blows away the moment you slow down. Approach at a steeper angle with a little more speed, and get the midships spring ashore quickly. Leaving is simple: let go and drift off.

Prop walk

Prop walk is the sideways kick of the propeller in reverse. On most charter yachts, with a right-handed propeller, the stern kicks to port when you go astern. Port side to, that helps: the kick brings the stern in as you stop. Starboard side to, it pulls the stern away, so come in at a shallower angle and use less astern. Check your boat in open water first.

Stopping and the first line

A yacht carries way (keeps moving) for longer than you expect. Come in at walking pace or less and stop her with a short burst of astern. If the approach looks wrong, go round and try again.

The first line ashore should stop the boat moving forward: the midships spring, led aft to a cleat or bollard. With it made fast and the engine slow ahead, the boat holds herself against the quay while the crew add bow and stern lines without hurry.

A touch of rudder sets the angle while you motor against the spring. Steering away from the quay brings the stern in. Steering towards it pushes the stern out. If you are staying, add fore and aft springs and adjust everything so she lies parallel.

Leaving: springing off

With the wind pushing you on, or boats close ahead and astern, use a spring to swing one end out first. Rig it as a slip line: led around the cleat or bollard ashore and back on board, so you can let go and pull it in from the boat. Brief the crew, then remove the other lines.

Springing the stern out

  1. Rig a fore spring as a slip line, from the bow led aft. Put a fender right at the bow.
  2. Motor gently ahead with the wheel turned to steer towards the quay. The spring holds the bow in and the stern swings out.
  3. When the stern is well clear, go into neutral, slip the spring and reverse away, allowing for prop walk.

Springing the bow out

  1. Rig an aft spring as a slip line, from the stern led forward. Put a fender on the quarter (the back corner of the boat).
  2. Motor gently astern with the rudder centred. The spring stops the boat going back and the bow swings out.
  3. When the bow is clear, go into neutral, slip the spring and motor ahead, wheel straight at first so the stern does not swing into the quay.

Going ahead sends strong propeller wash over the rudder, so springing the stern out is the more powerful method in a stronger onshore wind.

Watch out

Once the slip line is released, pull it in quickly and keep it out of the water. A trailing rope can wrap the propeller in seconds, just when you need the engine most.

Rafting up

When the quay is full, you may tie up alongside a boat that is already berthed. Good manners keep a raft friendly.

  • Ask first. Ask the crew of the boat you want to lie against, and rig fenders at the height of their topsides.
  • Bigger boats inside. Heavier boats should be nearest the quay.
  • Stagger the masts. If the boats roll, spreaders (the struts on the mast) level with each other can clash.
  • Lines to the boat and the shore. Rig breast lines and springs to the inner boat, then take your own bow and stern lines ashore, so the raft does not hang on the inner boat's cleats.
  • Cross by the foredeck. Walk over the foredeck, never through someone's cockpit, and quietly at night.
  • Plan the morning. If an inner boat leaves first, agree the plan the evening before.

Practise it on the water

On our courses you come alongside fuel docks, pontoons and town quays in different winds, port and starboard side to, until each crew member has helmed and handled the lines. You also spring off by the bow and by the stern, and raft up when a harbour is busy, so leaving a tight berth becomes routine.
