Before you arrive

Check-in usually starts in the late afternoon of the first day, once the base has cleaned and checked the boat after the last crew. You save time on the day if the paperwork is done before you travel:

  • Skipper licence. An IYT International Bareboat Skipper certificate, an ICC or an equivalent licence for the area. Some countries also ask for a VHF radio certificate, or for a second crew member with sailing experience. The charter company tells you what applies where you sail.
  • Crew list. Full names, dates of birth, nationality and passport numbers of everyone on board. Most bases ask for it a week or two in advance, because it goes on the boat's official papers.
  • Payment and deposit. The charter fee is usually paid in advance. The security deposit is paid or blocked on a credit card at check-in, unless you have bought a damage waiver.
  • Arrival time. Tell the base when you expect to arrive. If your flight is late, they can do the paperwork the next morning.

At the office

At the base office you show the licences and passports, sign the charter contract and leave the deposit. Read the contract before you sign. Look for the check-out time, the areas where you may sail, any night sailing rules and what the deposit covers.

Ask which papers are on board. You need the registration, insurance and crew list, plus any cruising permit or transit log the country requires. Keep them together in one folder at the chart table. Port police and coastguard may ask for them in any harbour.

Also ask for the base's phone number for breakdowns, and save it on two phones.

The base briefing

A member of the base staff walks you through the boat. Take notes or film it on your phone, because every boat is set up a little differently. Make sure you are shown:

  • The engine: how to start and stop it, where the dipstick, raw water strainer and fuel filter are.
  • The electrics: battery switches, shore power, the main panel and the charger.
  • Water and gas: water tanks and pump, the gas bottle, its valve and the cooker.
  • Toilets and holding tank: how to pump, and how to open and close the seacocks.
  • The anchor windlass, its breaker switch and remote control.
  • Instruments, chartplotter, autopilot and VHF radio.
  • Where the seacocks, bilge pumps, spare impeller and tools are.

Your own inspection

The base gives you a checklist and an inventory. Go through them yourself, with a crew member, rather than ticking boxes while the staff talk. It takes about an hour, and it is the last chance to have anything fixed or replaced before you sail.

Hull, deck and rigging

  • Hull and deck. Walk round the boat and look along the topsides for scratches, dents and gelcoat chips, especially at the bow and the stern.
  • Sails. Unroll the genoa and, if the wind allows, the mainsail. Look for tears, worn stitching and chafe. Check that the reefing lines run freely.
  • Rigging. Shrouds and forestay tight, split pins and rings in place at the deck fittings. Halyards and sheets not chafed, winches turning, two winch handles on board.

Engine, anchor and dinghy

  • Engine. Do the morning checks from the engine checks guide in this series, then start it and see cooling water at the exhaust. Try ahead and astern at the berth.
  • Anchor and windlass. The windlass works up and down, and the chain is marked every 10 metres. Ask how long the chain is, and check that its end is tied inside the chain locker.
  • Dinghy and outboard. No soft tubes or leaks, pump, oars and painter on board. Start the outboard and ask about its fuel.

Safety gear

  • Life jackets for everyone on board, in the right sizes, including children's. Harnesses and jackstays if the boat has them.
  • Liferaft with its service date in date, and how it is released.
  • Flares in date, fire extinguishers and a fire blanket, first aid kit, horn, torch, and the lifebuoy with its light.

Inventory and tanks

  • Lines and fenders. Count the mooring lines and fenders against the inventory, and find the boathook and the gangway.
  • Charts and pilot book for the area, and a chartplotter that works.
  • Tanks. Fuel and water should be full. Look at the gauges and ask when the boat was last filled. Check there is a full spare gas bottle.
  • Galley and cabins. Pots, cutlery and crockery for the whole crew, bedding and towels if they are included.

Recording damage

At check-out, the base compares the boat with the check-in list. Anything not written down may be charged to your deposit, even if it was there before you. So:

  • Photograph or film every scratch, dent, tear and missing item, close up and with part of the boat for scale. Your phone records the date and time.
  • Have the base staff write each fault on the check-in list, and keep a copy or a photo of it.
  • Ask for anything that matters for safety or sailing to be fixed before you leave: a torn sail, a windlass that does not work, missing life jackets.
Watch out

Do not sign the check-in list until the faults you found are on it. Once you sign, the boat is considered handed over in the condition written there.

Briefing the crew

Before you leave the berth, gather the whole crew for ten minutes. Show them the life jackets and how to put them on, the liferaft, the fire extinguishers and the first aid kit. Explain what to do if someone falls overboard, how to use the VHF radio to call for help, how to turn off the gas and how the toilets work. Agree who handles the lines when you leave.

The safety on board and man overboard guides in this series cover the crew briefing in more detail.

Check-out

Return on the evening before the end of the charter, at the time in your contract. Fill up with fuel on the way in, because most bases charge for an empty tank and fuel docks get busy on the last afternoon. At many bases a diver then checks the hull, keel, rudder and propeller, and the staff go through the inventory again. Report any damage or breakage yourself, rather than letting the staff find it. When all is in order, the base releases the deposit.
