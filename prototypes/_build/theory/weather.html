The Beaufort scale

The Beaufort scale describes wind strength in "forces" from 0 to 12. Forecasts in Greece, Turkey and Croatia often give wind in Beaufort, while apps usually show knots (nautical miles per hour). You need to switch between the two without thinking.

The table covers the range a charter skipper meets in a normal season. The knots are the mean (average) wind, not the gusts. The last column is a rough guide for a typical 40-foot (12-metre) cruising yacht with a sensible crew. Your own limits depend on the boat, the crew and the sea.

The sea state for a given force also depends on fetch (the distance the wind has blown over open water) and on how long it has been blowing. A Force 6 in a sheltered bay is a very different thing from a Force 6 after two days across the open Aegean.

Where to get a forecast

Use more than one source and compare them. If they disagree, plan for the stronger one.

  • National weather services. The Hellenic National Meteorological Service (Greece), the Turkish State Meteorological Service and the Croatian Meteorological and Hydrological Service all publish marine forecasts by sea area, usually with English versions. These are the official forecasts, and they carry the gale warnings.
  • NAVTEX. A small receiver on board that prints or displays safety messages, including weather forecasts and gale warnings, from coastal stations. The international service on 518 kHz is in English. You select the stations for your area.
  • VHF coast stations. Coast radio stations announce weather broadcasts on channel 16, then read them on a working channel. In Croatia there are continuous recorded forecasts on dedicated VHF channels. The pilot book (the sailing guide for the area) lists the channels and times.
  • Grib-based apps. A GRIB file is raw output from a computer weather model, shown as arrows or colours on a map. Apps are quick and visual, and you can compare several models. But models smooth out local effects, so they often under-read the wind near capes, in channels and below high land.

Reading the forecast

Wind arrows

Wind is always named after the direction it comes from. A north wind blows from the north towards the south. On most apps the arrow flies with the wind, so it points to where the wind is going. Some apps use wind barbs instead: the shaft points into the wind, and each long feather is 10 knots, each short one 5 knots.

Mean wind and gusts

The headline wind speed is the mean wind, averaged over about ten minutes. Gusts are short bursts above that. In the Mediterranean summer, gusts are often a third to a half stronger than the mean, and more in the lee of high islands. A forecast of "18 knots, gusts 28" means you should rig the boat for 28.

Look at the trend through the day, not just one number. Check the time the wind is expected to build and the time it should ease, and plan your passage around them.

Local winds

Meltemi

The meltemi is the summer wind of the Aegean. It blows from roughly May to September, strongest and most regular in July and August. It comes from the north-east in the north Aegean, from the north in the Cyclades, and turns north-west towards the Dodecanese and the south-west coast of Turkey.

It often starts light in the morning, builds towards midday and peaks in the afternoon. It may ease in the evening, but in a strong spell it can blow Force 6–7 day and night for several days. It comes with clear blue skies, so there are no clouds to warn you. Experienced Aegean skippers sail early, before it fills in, and choose routes that keep the wind behind the beam (off the side or from behind).

Sirocco

The sirocco is a warm south to south-east wind from North Africa. By the time it reaches Greece, Italy or Croatia it is humid and hazy, sometimes carrying reddish dust. In the Adriatic it is called the jugo. It usually builds over a day or more, and the long fetch raises a big, long swell. It is more common in spring and autumn.

Bora

The bora (bura in Croatian) is a cold north-east wind in the Adriatic that falls down the coastal mountains. It is very gusty and can arrive within minutes, sometimes from a clear sky. In the worst places, such as the Velebit Channel, gusts can exceed 50 knots. It is mostly a winter wind but can appear in summer. A cap of cloud sitting on the mountain tops is a classic warning sign. Croatian forecasts name the bora directly, so read them every morning.

Reef early

Reefing means making the sails smaller. The old rule holds: the first time you wonder whether to reef, reef. A reef put in early is calm and easy. A reef put in late, with the boat over on her side and the crew tired, is how things break and people get hurt.

  • If the forecast is Force 5 or more, reef before you leave harbour. You can always shake it out.
  • Most charter yachts have a furling mainsail and a roller genoa (the large front sail). Ease the sheet to take the load off before you roll a sail in.
  • Reef for the gusts, not the mean wind.
  • Before rounding a cape or entering a channel, reduce sail first. Wind accelerates there.

Go or no go

Before each passage, the skipper makes a clear decision, and says it out loud to the crew. Work through these questions:

  1. What is the mean wind and the gust forecast, and when does it peak?
  2. Which direction is it from? Will you be beating (sailing towards the wind) or running with it?
  3. Are there exposed stretches, capes or channels on the route?
  4. Is the crew rested, experienced and not seasick? Is the boat in good order?
  5. Is the destination sheltered from this wind? What is your plan B if conditions get worse?
  6. Does your charter agreement set any wind limits?

If the honest answer to any of these worries you, wait. Waiting a day in harbour costs very little. Many good skippers simply leave at dawn and are tied up before the afternoon wind arrives.

Watch out

High islands make their own gusts. In a meltemi, wind pours down the lee (sheltered) side of islands such as Andros, Tinos or Evia and hits the water in violent, shifting gusts, often much stronger than the forecast. The water close in may look calm. Watch for dark patches racing across the surface, reef before you get there, and give high headlands a wide berth.
