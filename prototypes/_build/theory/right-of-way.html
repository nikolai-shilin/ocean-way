Lookout and safe speed

The International Regulations for Preventing Collisions at Sea, known as the COLREGs, apply to every vessel, from a cruise ship to a dinghy; some ports add local rules on top. They begin with what everyone must do all the time.

Rule 5, lookout. Every vessel must keep a proper lookout at all times, by sight and hearing and by every other available means, such as radar and AIS (which shows ships' positions on a screen). On a yacht, check the whole horizon regularly, including behind the headsail.

Rule 6, safe speed. Every vessel must go at a speed that lets her take proper action to avoid a collision and stop within a suitable distance. There is no fixed number. It depends on visibility, traffic, how quickly your boat stops and turns, wind, sea and current, shore lights at night and the depth under the keel.

Is there a risk of collision?

Rule 7 asks you to decide early whether a risk of collision exists. The simplest test is the compass bearing. Take a bearing of the other vessel with a hand-bearing compass, or line her up with a stanchion (an upright guardrail post) while holding a steady course, then repeat after a minute or two.

  • If the bearing stays steady and the vessel is getting closer, you are on a collision course.
  • If the bearing moves clearly ahead or astern, you will probably pass clear.

With large ships, tows or at close range, a risk can exist even when the bearing changes. If in doubt, assume the risk exists. Never decide on a single look or radar reading.

How to take action

Rule 8 says avoiding action must be taken in ample time, be large enough to be obvious to the other vessel by eye or by radar, and leave you passing at a safe distance. Avoid a series of small changes, and keep checking until she is past and clear.

With sea room, a large change of course alone is often best; you can also slow down, stop or go astern (reverse). A clear turn to pass well astern of a ship shows your intention; a nudge of a few degrees does not.

Two sailing vessels

Rule 12 applies when two vessels, both under sail alone, approach each other. With the wind on your port (left) side you are on port tack. The windward side is the side opposite the mainsail.

  1. Wind on different sides: the vessel on port tack keeps out of the way of the one on starboard tack.
  2. Wind on the same side: the windward vessel (the one further upwind) keeps out of the way of the leeward one.
  3. Cannot tell: on port tack, if you see a vessel to windward and cannot tell her tack, keep out of the way.

Overtaking, head-on and crossing

Rule 13, overtaking. Any vessel overtaking another keeps out of the way, whatever type either vessel is. This overrides Rules 12 and 18: a sailing yacht overtaking a motor boat keeps clear of it. You are overtaking if you approach from more than 22.5° abaft her beam (behind the line across her middle), where at night you would see only her stern light. If in doubt, assume you are overtaking.

Rule 14, head-on. When two power-driven vessels meet on reciprocal or nearly reciprocal courses, each turns to starboard so they pass port side to port side.

Rule 15, crossing. When two power-driven vessels cross, the one that has the other on her own starboard side gives way and, if she can, avoids crossing ahead. In short: if you see her red port sidelight, you give way.

Give-way and stand-on duties

Rule 16: the give-way vessel takes early and substantial action to keep well clear, ideally passing astern rather than crossing ahead.

Rule 17: the stand-on vessel keeps her course and speed. That is a duty, not a privilege: the give-way vessel relies on you being predictable. If she is clearly not taking proper action, sound at least five short, rapid blasts (Rule 34); you may also act on your own. If she is so close that her action alone cannot prevent a collision, you must act. A power-driven stand-on vessel should then avoid turning to port for a vessel on her own port side. None of this frees the give-way vessel from her duty.

Who gives way to whom

Rule 18 sets the order between different kinds of vessel, except where Rules 9, 10 (traffic separation schemes) and 13 say otherwise. Broadly, the less able a vessel is to manoeuvre, the more others keep clear of her.

  • A power-driven vessel keeps out of the way of a vessel not under command (unable to manoeuvre, for example after a breakdown), a vessel restricted in her ability to manoeuvre (such as a dredger at work), a vessel engaged in fishing, and a sailing vessel.
  • A sailing vessel keeps out of the way of the first three.
  • Where possible, every vessel avoids impeding a vessel constrained by her draught (a deep ship limited to the deep water).

"Engaged in fishing" means with nets, lines or trawls that restrict her movement; a boat trolling a line does not count. A sailing vessel is one under sail with any engine not in use. Once the engine drives the propeller, a sailing yacht is a power-driven vessel, even with every sail set.

Watch out

A motorsailing yacht follows the rules for a power-driven vessel, so she gives way to sailing yachts. By day she shows a cone, point down, forward where it can best be seen. At night she shows a steaming light (a white masthead light) with her sidelights and stern light, never the tricolour at the masthead. Others judge you by what they see: without the cone, a motorsailing yacht looks like a sailing vessel standing on.

Narrow channels and big ships

Rule 9 covers narrow channels and fairways, such as a buoyed harbour approach. Keep as near to the outer edge of the channel on your starboard side as is safe and practicable. A vessel under 20 metres long, or any sailing vessel, must not impede the passage of a vessel that can safely navigate only inside the channel. Do not cross a channel if that would impede such a vessel, and avoid anchoring in it.

"Must not impede" means acting early, before any risk of collision develops. A large ship may need well over a mile to stop, and from her bridge a yacht close ahead can be hidden under the bow. Near busy ferry ports such as Piraeus or Bodrum, assume the ship has not seen you. If you must cross, cross quickly and at right angles. If a ship sounds five or more short blasts, she doubts you are keeping clear: act at once.

Practise it on the water

On the International Bareboat Skipper course, each time another vessel appears you take bearings, decide who gives way and say it aloud before you act. You also practise identifying vessels by their lights and shapes.
