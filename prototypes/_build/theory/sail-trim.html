The controls on a cruising yacht

Every sail has an angle to the wind and a shape. The sheets set the angle. The other lines set the shape: how deep or flat the sail is, and how much the top falls away. A charter yacht of 11 to 14 metres has these controls, most of them led to the cockpit.

  • Mainsheet. Pulls the boom in and out. Close-hauled it also pulls the boom down, which tightens the leech (the back edge of the sail).
  • Traveller. A car on a track that the mainsheet is attached to. It moves the boom sideways without changing the leech tension much. Some charter yachts have none.
  • Genoa sheets and cars. Each genoa sheet runs through a car, a sliding block on a track on the side deck. Moving the car forward pulls the sheet down more and tightens the leech. Moving it aft lets the top of the sail open.
  • Halyards. The lines that hoist the sails. More tension on the luff (the front edge) moves the deepest part of the sail forward and flattens the back of it.
  • Outhaul. Pulls the foot (bottom edge) of the main out along the boom. Tight makes the lower part flat; eased makes it deeper and more powerful.
  • Kicker. Also called the vang. It holds the boom down when the mainsheet is eased, so the main keeps its shape off the wind.
  • Backstay. The wire from the masthead to the stern, fixed on most charter yachts. If yours has an adjuster, tightening it flattens the main and stops the forestay sagging. Ease it off the wind.

An in-mast furling main has no horizontal battens, so it is flatter. You trim it the same way, but the outhaul matters more.

Reading the telltales on the genoa and the main

Genoa telltales are short ribbons on both sides of the sail, near the luff, usually in three pairs. As the points of sail article explains, when both sides stream back the trim is right. Comparing the three pairs also tells you where the car should be.

SignalLikely causeWhat to do
Windward telltales lift at every heightSheet too far out, or steering too close to the windSheet in, or bear away a little
Leeward telltales lift at every heightSheet too tight, or steering too far off the windEase the sheet, or head up a little

To test it, head up slowly: with the car in the right place, all three windward telltales lift together.

On the main, look at the leech telltales, the ribbons at the end of each batten. The lower ones should stream back almost all the time. The top one should stream most of the time and only now and then curl round behind the sail. If it stays hidden on the leeward side, the leech is too tight: ease the mainsheet or the kicker a little.

Twist and the shape of the sail

Twist is how much the top of a sail falls away to leeward compared with the bottom. Some twist is right, because the wind high up the mast is stronger and comes from slightly further aft. Too little and the top stalls; too much and it flaps uselessly.

  • Where to set it. On the main, twist is set by the mainsheet when close-hauled and by the kicker off the wind. On the genoa, it is set by the car.
  • In light wind, ease the outhaul and halyards a little for a deeper sail, and keep some twist so the air can flow off the leech.
  • In a medium breeze, close the leech a little for the best pointing upwind.
  • In strong wind, flatten the sails with outhaul, halyard and backstay, and let the top twist open to spill the gusts. When that is not enough, it is time to reef.

How trim changes from close-hauled to a run

As you bear away, ease the sheets. The shape needs changing too, because an eased sheet lets the boom and the genoa clew (the bottom back corner) rise, so the sails twist open.

On a run, too much twist in the main makes the boat roll, and a firm kicker cures it. A genoa hidden behind the main only slats and wears, so pole it out or roll it away.

Weather helm and balance

Weather helm is the boat's wish to turn into the wind. A little is good: with about 3 to 5 degrees of rudder she steers with a light touch and heads up by herself if you let go. Heavy weather helm means the rudder is working as a brake, and in a gust she may round up (turn into the wind out of control).

The usual causes are too much heel, a main that is too full or sheeted too hard, and too little headsail. Cruising yachts sail best at 15 to 20 degrees of heel. Work through the fixes in order:

  1. Ease the traveller down to leeward, or ease the mainsheet in the gusts.
  2. Flatten the main with the outhaul, halyard and backstay if you have one.
  3. Let the main twist a little more.
  4. Reef the main, as described in the reefing article.
Tip

In the Saronic Gulf the summer wind is often light in the morning and builds to force 4 or 5 in the afternoon, with strong gusts off the high ground of Aegina, Poros and Hydra. Flatten the sails before you reach the gusty stretch, not once you are in it.

Heaving-to: stopping under sail

Heaving-to parks the boat at sea. The backed genoa pushes the bow away from the wind, while the main and the rudder push it back up. She settles about 50 to 60 degrees off the wind, drifting slowly sideways, and the motion becomes calm.

  1. Sail close-hauled, with plenty of sea room to leeward (downwind).
  2. Tack, but do not release the genoa sheet. The genoa is now held on the windward side.
  3. Ease the mainsheet or the traveller until the boat stops trying to sail forward.
  4. Turn the wheel as if to head up into the wind, and lock it with the wheel brake. With a tiller, lash it to leeward.
  5. Adjust the main until she lies quietly. Roll away some genoa if it presses hard on the spreaders.

To sail on, release the windward genoa sheet, sheet in on the leeward side and straighten the wheel.

Heave-to to reef in peace, to eat lunch, to sort out a tangle, or to wait outside a harbour for daylight or a free berth. Choose starboard tack if you can, because it has right of way over port tack. Some modern yachts with short fin keels will not lie still, so try yours on a calm day first.

Watch out

A hove-to boat drifts steadily to leeward and is still under way. Keep a lookout, check that the drift takes you away from land and shipping, and show navigation lights for a sailing vessel at night.

Trimming with a small crew

  • Set up early. Move the genoa car and flatten the main before a tack or a gust, while the sails are quiet.
  • Use the autopilot. Let it steer while you trim, and switch it off before a tack or a gybe.
  • Make the genoa smaller. A genoa rolled in by a third is much easier to tack single-handed, and often costs little speed in a breeze.
  • Pull in before it loads. In a tack, take in the new sheet fast by hand while the sail crosses, then winch only the last part.
  • Electric winches. Keep fingers clear, and stop at once if something sounds wrong.

Practise it on the water

On our courses each crew member trims the genoa and the main on every point of sail, with the instructor asking what each telltale says and which control to change. You heave-to on the first days, reef while hove-to, and sail the boat with two people on deck so you know you can manage her short-handed.
