Why there are tides

Tides are the regular rise and fall of the sea, caused mainly by the pull of the moon and, to a lesser extent, the sun. The moon draws the oceans into a bulge on the side of the Earth facing it, and a second bulge forms on the far side. As the Earth turns, coasts pass through both.

So on most Atlantic and North Sea coasts there are two high waters and two low waters in a little over 24 hours, about 50 minutes later each day. From low water to high water takes roughly six hours.

  • High water (HW). The highest level the tide reaches on one cycle.
  • Low water (LW). The lowest level on one cycle.
  • Range. The difference in height between a high water and the next low water. A big range means more water moving and stronger streams.

Springs and neaps

At new moon and full moon, the sun and moon line up with the Earth and their pulls add together. You get spring tides: higher high waters, lower low waters, a bigger range and stronger streams. The name has nothing to do with the season.

At half moon they pull at right angles and partly cancel, giving neap tides with a small range and weaker streams. The cycle from springs to neaps and back takes about two weeks, and the biggest springs usually come a day or two after full or new moon.

Chart datum and drying heights

Chart datum (CD). The level from which charted depths are measured. On most modern charts it is Lowest Astronomical Tide (LAT), the lowest level the tide is predicted to reach in average weather. So there is usually more water than the chart shows, rarely less.

Charted depth. A depth printed in the sea, measured down from chart datum.

Drying height. Banks and mudflats that are uncovered at chart datum show an underlined figure: their height above chart datum.

Height of tide. How far the sea surface is above chart datum at a given moment. It ties everything together:

  • Depth of water = charted depth + height of tide.
  • Depth over a drying area = height of tide − drying height.

Heights of land, lights and bridge clearances are measured from a high-water level instead. The chart notes say which.

Tide tables and ports

Tide tables in almanacs and apps give the daily times and heights of high and low water. They are predictions, and weather can move the real level away from them.

Standard ports. The main harbours, with full daily predictions and a tidal curve (a graph of height between low and high water).

Secondary ports. Smaller harbours, worked out by applying the almanac's time and height differences to a nearby standard port.

Check the time zone. Many tables use UT (Universal Time), so add the difference for local or summer time.

The rule of twelfths

The tide rises slowly at first, fastest in the middle, then slowly again. The rule of twelfths estimates the height each hour, assuming about six hours from low to high water. Divide the range into twelve parts. Hour by hour, the tide rises by 1, 2, 3, 3, 2 and 1 twelfths, and falls in the same pattern after high water.

Worked example: low water is at 0800, height 1.0 metre. High water is at 1400, height 5.8 metres. The range is 4.8 metres, so one twelfth is 0.4 metres.

Clearance and crossing a bar

Many harbours have a bar (a bank of sand or shingle across the entrance) you can only cross with enough tide.

  1. Find the charted depth over the bar. Say it is 0.7 metres.
  2. Take your draught (the depth of the keel below the waterline). Say 1.9 metres.
  3. Add an under-keel clearance (safety margin). Use at least a metre over a bar, more in swell, because the boat drops into each trough.
  4. So you need 1.9 + 1.0 = 2.9 metres of water. The bar already has 0.7 metres at chart datum, so the height of tide must be at least 2.9 − 0.7 = 2.2 metres.

From the table, the height of tide reaches 2.2 metres at 1000. If the falling tide mirrors the rise, it drops back to 2.2 metres at about 1800. So the window is roughly 1000 to 1800. Aim for the middle of it, not the edges.

Tidal streams

As the tide rises and falls, water flows along the coast and in and out of estuaries. This is the tidal stream. The flood runs while the tide is rising, the ebb while it is falling, and the pause between them is slack water. A stream is described by its set (the direction it flows towards, the opposite of how wind is named) and its rate in knots.

  • Tidal diamonds. A letter in a diamond on the chart. A table on the chart gives the set and rate there for each hour before and after high water at a standard port, at springs and at neaps.
  • Tidal stream atlases. A booklet with a chartlet for each hour of the tide. Arrows show direction, and figures such as 08,15 give the rate in tenths of a knot: 0.8 knots at neaps, 1.5 at springs.

Spring rates are often around twice neap rates. Streams are strongest off headlands and in narrow channels, and do not always turn at high or low water.

Wind against tide

When the wind blows against the stream, the waves become short, steep and close together. A Force 5 that is pleasant with the tide can be rough against a spring ebb, and off headlands you may meet overfalls (breaking water where the stream runs over an uneven bottom). Round headlands at slack water or with wind and stream together.

Tides in our home waters

In most of the Mediterranean and Aegean, including the Turkish coast and the Greek islands, the tidal range is small, typically under half a metre. It is larger in a few places: close to a metre in the northern Adriatic and up to around two metres in the Gulf of Gabès in Tunisia.

So here, other things usually matter more. Strong wind can pile water against a coast or push it away. Low pressure raises the sea and high pressure lowers it, by roughly a centimetre per hectopascal. Currents run through straits, and a long meltemi sets up a surface drift.
