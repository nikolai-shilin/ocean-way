A few rope words

On a yacht, a rope with a job is called a line: sheets control the sails, halyards hoist them. Knot instructions use a few words:

  • Working end: the free end you tie with.
  • Standing part: the rest of the line, usually the part that takes the load.
  • Loop and bight: a loop is where the line crosses itself; a bight is a U-shaped fold that does not cross.

A good knot is quick to tie, holds under load and comes undone afterwards.

Bowline

What it is for: a fixed loop at the end of a line that does not slip or tighten. You use it to put a loop in a mooring line to drop over a bollard, to tie a sheet to the clew (back corner) of a headsail, or to make a loop to go round a person.

  1. Make a small loop in the standing part, with the part leading to the working end lying on top.
  2. Pass the working end up through the small loop from underneath.
  3. Take it round behind the standing part.
  4. Bring it back down through the small loop, alongside itself.
  5. Hold the working end and the side of the big loop together, and pull the standing part to tighten.

How to check it: the working end finishes inside the big loop, a collar wraps round the standing part, and the tail is at least a hand's width long.

Common mistake: making the first small loop the wrong way round, with the standing part on top. The knot then pulls straight out. A bowline also cannot be untied under load, and it can shake loose on a flogging sheet.

Clove hitch

What it is for: a quick, adjustable hitch (a knot that ties a line to an object). Its everyday job is hanging fenders from the guardrail (the wire round the deck edge), where you can quickly adjust their height.

  1. Pass the working end over the rail and round it.
  2. Cross the end over its own standing part and take a second turn round the rail, beside the first.
  3. Tuck the working end under this second turn, so it comes out between the two turns.
  4. Pull both parts to tighten.

How to check it: two neat turns side by side, a diagonal cross over the top, and both ends coming out of the middle in opposite directions.

Common mistake: trusting it with a load that keeps working back and forth: it can slip or roll off. For fenders left out for a while, add a half hitch round the standing part.

Round turn and two half hitches

What it is for: tying a line to a ring, rail or post that takes a steady load, such as a mooring ring on a harbour wall or the painter (bow line) of the dinghy. You can tie and untie it under strain, because the round turn takes the load.

  1. Take the working end twice round the ring or post, so the line wraps it completely.
  2. Bring the end over the standing part and tuck it through, making a half hitch.
  3. Make a second half hitch the same way, a little further along the standing part.
  4. Slide both hitches up snug against the round turn.

How to check it: the two half hitches together look like a clove hitch round the standing part, with a decent tail left over.

Common mistake: tying the second half hitch in the opposite direction to the first. It looks similar but is much less secure. Both hitches must go round the same way.

Cleat hitch

What it is for: making fast (securing) a line on a cleat, the two-horned fitting on deck and on pontoons. Use it for mooring lines and halyards.

  1. Lead the line to the far horn of the cleat first, the one furthest from where the load comes from.
  2. Take a full turn round the base of the cleat, under both horns.
  3. Cross over the top and round the opposite horn to make a figure of eight. One or two figures of eight are enough.
  4. Finish with a locking hitch: twist a small loop so the line lies under itself, drop it over a horn and pull it tight. The tail should lie parallel with the turn below it.

How to check it: the crossings are neat and lie on top of each other, and the line leads straight onto the cleat without rubbing on its edge.

Common mistake: piling on extra turns and hitches. A tangle jams under load and cannot be released fast. Some skippers want no locking hitch on sheets for the same reason; follow the skipper's way.

Figure-of-eight stopper

What it is for: a stopper knot in the end of a line, so it cannot run out through a block (pulley), clutch or fairlead. Tie one in the tail of each headsail sheet, so a sheet let go in a hurry cannot run away forward.

  1. Take the working end over the standing part to make a loop.
  2. Pass it round behind the standing part.
  3. Bring it back down through the loop from the front.
  4. Pull tight, leaving a short tail.

How to check it: it looks like the figure 8 and is clearly too fat to pass through the block.

Common mistake: tying a simple overhand knot instead. It is smaller, can jam so hard that it has to be cut, and may pull through a large block.

Reef knot, and its limits

What it is for: tying the two ends of the same line together round something soft. Examples are the reef points (short ties) that gather the bundle of a reefed mainsail, and sail ties.

  1. Take the left end over and under the right.
  2. Take the end that is now on the right over and under the left.
  3. Pull both ends to tighten.

How to check it: the knot lies flat and symmetrical, and each end lies alongside its own standing part, both on the same side.

Common mistake: crossing the same way twice, which makes a granny knot. It slips or jams.

Watch out

Never use a reef knot to join two lines that will take a load, especially lines of different thickness. It can capsize (spill into a shape that slides apart) without warning. To join two lines, learn the sheet bend. When reefing, tie reef points round the sail only, not round the boom, so the sail does not tear if the reefing line fails.

Coiling and handing over a line

After you make fast, tidy the tail so it runs free next time.

  • Coiling: work from the fixed end towards the free end, making even loops in one hand. Three-strand rope is usually coiled clockwise. Braided rope kinks in round coils, so lay it in figure-of-eight loops or flake it (let it fall loosely so it runs without twisting).
  • Hanging a halyard tail: reach through the coil, pull a bight of the line from near the cleat back through it, twist it and hang it over the top horn of the cleat.
  • Handing over: tell the person on the quay what you want, for example "Bow line, round the bollard and back to me." Make sure the end on board is made fast, pass or throw the line in a loose coil, and never wrap a loaded line round your hand.

Practise it on the water

On the Introduction to Yachting course you tie these knots for real jobs: fenders on before entering harbour, sheets on the headsail, lines ashore when you moor. You practise until you can tie each one without looking, as you will need to at night.
