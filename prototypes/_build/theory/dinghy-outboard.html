The tender and its kit

Most charter yachts of 11 to 14 metres come with an inflatable tender (the dinghy) of about 2.5 to 3 metres and a small petrol outboard of 2 to 6 horsepower. In the Saronic Gulf you use it every day, to reach a taverna or a beach from the anchorage.

If you have to inflate it yourself, close all the valves, then pump each chamber in turn with the foot pump, a little at a time, so no single tube takes all the strain. Pump until the tubes feel hard under your thumb, about 0.2 bar if the pump has a gauge. Check the tubes again each morning.

Before the first trip, check that the dinghy has oars that fit the rowlocks, a pump, a bailer, a painter (the line at the bow) of at least 4 metres, and a cable and padlock. Fuel goes in a proper can, with the right oil mix if the outboard is a 2-stroke.

Towing the dinghy or lifting it aboard

On short hops in calm water, most crews tow the dinghy. Let out enough painter for it to ride on the back of the second wave behind the stern, usually 10 to 15 metres. Take everything loose out of it first.

Shorten the painter before you slow down, go astern or enter harbour. A long, slack painter is the classic way to wrap a line round your own propeller.

In wind and waves a towed dinghy can fill with water, flip over or break its painter. Before a passage in Force 5 or more, or an open crossing, lift it aboard. Catamarans and some monohulls have davits (small cranes at the stern) to hoist it. On most monohulls you take off the outboard, haul the dinghy onto the foredeck with a halyard, let out some air and lash it down upside down.

Watch out

Never tow the dinghy with the outboard on it. If the dinghy flips, the engine goes under water and is ruined. Put the outboard on its bracket on the yacht's stern rail before you leave.

Fitting and removing the outboard

A small outboard weighs 13 to 25 kilograms and sinks at once if it slips.

  1. Pull the dinghy alongside the stern platform and tie it short at the bow and the stern.
  2. One person climbs into the dinghy first and sits in the middle, near the transom (the flat board at the back).
  3. Tie a safety line from the outboard to a cleat on the yacht before you lift it off its bracket.
  4. Hand the outboard down, tilted up, and lower it onto the transom with its clamp centred.
  5. Tighten both clamp screws by hand and tie the outboard's own lanyard to the dinghy. Only then untie the safety line.

To remove it, tie the safety line to the yacht first, then loosen the clamps. Close the fuel tap and the tank vent so petrol does not leak. Lay a 4-stroke outboard down only on the side shown on its label, or oil runs into the cylinder.

Starting the outboard

  1. Lower the engine into the water and lock it down.
  2. Open the vent screw on the tank cap and turn the fuel tap on.
  3. Clip the kill cord (the cut-out lanyard) to your wrist or lifejacket. Most outboards with a kill cord will not start without its clip in place.
  4. If there is a gear lever, put it in neutral. Set the twist-grip throttle to the start mark.
  5. Pull the choke out if the engine is cold.
  6. Pull the starter cord slowly until you feel resistance, then give one long, firm pull.
  7. Once it runs, push the choke in slowly and check that a thin jet of cooling water comes out of the tell-tale (a small hole at the back of the engine).

To stop, press the stop button or pull the kill cord. Close the fuel tap if you leave the dinghy for a while.

Loading, lifejackets and lights

A plate on the transom gives the maximum number of people and load. A typical 3-metre tender takes three or four adults, and in a chop that is too many. Make two trips rather than one wet, overloaded one.

  • Board one at a time. Step into the middle of the dinghy, not onto a tube, holding the yacht with one hand.
  • Sit low and spread out. Never stand, and keep the weight balanced side to side.
  • Wear a lifejacket. In the dinghy everyone wears one. Many dinghy accidents happen climbing in or out late in the evening, after dinner ashore.
  • Carry a torch after dark. A dinghy at night is almost invisible. Under the COLREGs a small boat under oars must have a white light ready to show in time to avoid a collision, and a torch is the practical answer under engine too.

Driving in an anchorage

A swimmer's head is very hard to see from a dinghy, and an outboard propeller causes terrible injuries.

  • Go no faster than walking pace, about 3 knots, and leave no wake among anchored boats.
  • Keep well clear of swimmers and snorkellers, and give a wide berth to any boat flying a diver flag.
  • Many Greek beaches have a swimming area marked by yellow buoys. Keep outside it, or use a marked lane at the slowest speed.
  • Stop the engine when you come alongside the yacht and anyone is in the water near the stern.

Landing on a beach or a quay

On a beach. Come in slowly and straight. A few metres out, stop the engine and tilt it up before the propeller touches the bottom. The crew steps out into knee-deep water, and together you carry the dinghy well above the waterline. Tie the painter to a rock or a tree, because the wash of a passing ferry can float it away.

On a quay. Approach slowly, into the wind if you can. One person holds the painter and steps ashore once the dinghy is still. Nobody jumps. Never block steps or a ladder, and keep out of the spaces used by ferries and fishing boats.

At a crowded dinghy dock. In Poros, Aegina or Ermioni on a summer evening, ten dinghies may share a few metres of quay. Push in gently bow first, tilt the engine up so your propeller cannot damage the dinghies beside you, and tie up with a long painter so others can move your dinghy aside.

Theft and the night

Dinghies and outboards are stolen from Greek harbours every season, mostly at night. Many charter contracts do not cover a stolen dinghy or outboard unless it was locked.

  • Ashore, run the cable through the outboard's clamp handles and the dinghy's handle, and lock it to a ring or a bollard.
  • At night, lift the dinghy out of the water, on the davits or beside the yacht with a halyard. It is much harder to steal there.
  • If the dinghy stays in the water, take the outboard back onto its bracket on the yacht.

When the engine stops

ProblemLikely causeWhat to do
Will not startFuel tap or tank vent closed, kill cord clip missing, engine flooded with fuelOpen the tap and the vent and check the clip. If it is flooded, push the choke in and pull with the throttle fully open.
Runs, but the dinghy hardly movesBroken shear pin (a soft pin that breaks when the propeller hits a rock), weed or plastic on the propellerStop the engine, tilt it up and look. Fit the spare shear pin if you carry one.

If you cannot fix it quickly, row. An inflatable rows badly, so sit in the middle and use short, quick strokes. With two people, one can kneel at the bow and paddle on each side in turn.

The danger is an offshore wind. In summer the afternoon wind in the Saronic Gulf often reaches 15 to 20 knots, and gusts can fall off the hills at night. If you cannot make headway towards the yacht, row across the wind to the nearest shore and wait there. Call the yacht by phone or VHF so someone can come for you.

Tip

Tell someone on the yacht where you are going and when you will be back. In an offshore wind, never go without oars, a phone in a waterproof bag and a second person.

Practise it on the water

On our courses each crew member fits and removes the outboard, starts it and drives the dinghy to a beach and back. You also row a short distance with the engine tilted up, and lift the dinghy aboard as you would before a rough passage.
