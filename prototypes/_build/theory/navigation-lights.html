How the lights work

Part C of the COLREGs (the International Regulations for Preventing Collisions at Sea, Rules 20 to 31) sets out which lights and shapes every vessel must show. Lights are shown from sunset to sunrise and in restricted visibility. Shapes are their daytime equivalent: black balls, cones and diamonds.

Each light shines only over a fixed arc, measured from right ahead. Rule 21 defines them:

  • Masthead light: white, shining forward over 225°, from right ahead to 22.5° abaft (behind) the beam on each side. Despite the name, on a yacht it is usually fitted partway up the front of the mast. Sailors often call it the steaming light.
  • Sidelights: red on the port (left) side and green on the starboard (right) side, each covering 112.5°, from right ahead to 22.5° abaft the beam.
  • Sternlight: white, shining aft over 135°, 67.5° each side of right astern.
  • All-round light: any colour, visible over the full 360°.

The arcs fit together. The sternlight covers exactly the sector the sidelights and masthead light leave dark, so from any direction you see a combination that tells you which way the other vessel is pointing. See only green and you are looking at her starboard side.

Minimum ranges depend on size. For a vessel of 12 to 20 metres the masthead light must be visible at 3 miles and the sidelights and sternlight at 2 miles. Under 12 metres the figures drop to 2 miles for the masthead light and sternlight, and 1 mile for the sidelights. A small yacht's lights are dim. Do not assume a ship has seen you.

A sailing yacht under sail

Under Rule 25, a sailing vessel underway (not at anchor, made fast or aground) shows sidelights and a sternlight, and nothing else. No masthead light. The absence of a white light above the sidelights is what tells other vessels you are sailing.

A sailing vessel under 20 metres may instead combine the three into one tricolour lantern at or near the top of the mast. It is more visible over waves and uses less power, which is why most cruising yachts have one. You use either the tricolour or the deck-level lights, never both together.

Rule 25 also allows two optional all-round lights at the masthead, red over green, in addition to the deck-level sidelights and sternlight. They are rare on yachts and must not be combined with a tricolour.

Under engine and motorsailing

The moment the engine is in gear and driving the boat, you are a power-driven vessel under the rules, even with the sails up. You lose the sailing vessel's privileges and your lights must change:

  • Switch on the masthead light (steaming light).
  • Show sidelights and sternlight at deck level.
  • Switch off the tricolour. It may only be used under sail. A tricolour with a steaming light below it is a common mistake and gives a confusing picture.

By day, a vessel under sail that is also motoring must show a cone, point down, forward where it can best be seen (Rule 25(e)). Few yachts bother, but it is required.

At anchor and other day shapes

At anchor, a vessel under 50 metres shows one all-round white light where it can best be seen (Rule 30). By day she shows one black ball in the forepart. Vessels of 50 metres or more show two anchor lights, the forward one higher. In a crowded Greek or Croatian anchorage, an anchor light at the masthead can be hard to spot from a dinghy or from a boat coming in; many skippers add a light lower down as well.

Other day shapes worth knowing:

  • Two cones, points together: fishing.
  • Ball, diamond, ball: restricted in ability to manoeuvre.
  • Two balls: not under command (unable to manoeuvre, for example after an engine or steering failure).
  • A diamond: towing, where the tow is over 200 metres long.

Common sights at night

Power-driven vessels

A power-driven vessel under 50 metres shows a masthead light, sidelights and a sternlight. From right ahead you see white above red and green. From her starboard side, white and green. From her port side, white and red. From astern, a single white sternlight, which looks the same as a sailing vessel from astern.

A vessel of 50 metres or more must carry a second masthead light, further aft and higher than the first. The pair works like a gunsight. If the two white lights are in a vertical line with both sidelights visible, she is coming straight at you. As they open out, you can read which way she is turning.

Fishing vessels

Fishing vessels show two all-round lights in a vertical line. Green over white means trawling (dragging a net). Red over white means other fishing, such as nets or long lines. If they are making way through the water, they add sidelights and a sternlight. In the Aegean and along the Turkish coast, expect small fishing boats close inshore at night.

Restricted and towing

A vessel restricted in ability to manoeuvre by her work, such as a dredger, cable layer or a ship refuelling at sea, shows red, white, red all-round lights in a vertical line, plus normal steaming lights if making way. A vessel towing shows two masthead lights in a vertical line (three if the tow is over 200 metres), sidelights, sternlight and a yellow towing light above the sternlight. The tow behind her shows sidelights and a sternlight. Never pass between a tug and its tow.

The "what to do" column is a guide, not a substitute for the steering rules. Rule 18 says a sailing vessel keeps clear of vessels fishing, restricted in ability to manoeuvre and not under command. Whatever the rules say, if the compass bearing of another vessel's lights does not change while the range closes, there is a risk of collision. Act early.

Watch out

A big ship can be hard to judge at night. Her lights may sit high against shore lights, and she covers a mile in a few minutes. If in doubt, alter course early and by a large amount so the change is obvious, and call on VHF channel 16 if needed.

Practise it on the water

On our courses you practise this during night passages: identifying real lights from the cockpit, taking bearings on them and deciding who gives way. Before each night sail you also check your own lights and switch between tricolour and steaming light as you change from sail to engine.
