Why check the engine every day

A sailing yacht relies on its engine more than you might think: leaving and entering harbour, motoring through a calm, clawing off a lee shore (a coast the wind blows towards) or getting back to someone who has fallen overboard. Charter yachts usually have a small diesel of 30 to 60 horsepower, often a Yanmar or a Volvo Penta, under the companionway steps. Lift the steps or open the side panel and you can reach everything in this guide.

The base checks the engine before handover. After that it is the skipper's job. Do the checks below every morning before you start, with the engine cold. They take five minutes and catch most problems while you are still tied up.

Checks with the engine cold

  • Engine oil. Pull out the dipstick, wipe it, push it fully back in and pull it out again. The level should be between the two marks. Top up a little at a time through the filler cap on top of the engine. Milky oil, like coffee with cream, means water has got into it: do not run the engine, call the base.
  • Coolant. Most charter diesels have two cooling circuits. Fresh water with antifreeze (coolant) circulates inside the engine, and seawater (raw water) cools it through a heat exchanger. Check the coolant level in the header tank or the expansion bottle. Open the cap only when the engine is cold: hot coolant is under pressure and can scald.
  • Raw water seacock and strainer. The seacock (a valve on a hole through the hull) lets seawater in to cool the engine, and it must be open. The strainer next to it has a clear bowl. If you see weed, plastic or sand, close the seacock, clean the strainer, refit the lid tightly and open the seacock again.
  • Drive belt. The belt drives the alternator, which charges the batteries, and often the water pump too. Press it in the middle of its longest run: it should give by about a centimetre. Look for cracks, fraying or black dust around it.
  • Leaks and bilge. Look under and around the engine with a torch. Oil, diesel or water in the drip tray or the bilge (the lowest part of the hull) means something is leaking. A strong smell of diesel is a warning too.
  • Fuel filter. Many engines have a pre-filter with a clear bowl that separates water from the fuel. Water and dirt sink to the bottom. A drop or two is normal and can be drained off. A lot of water means bad fuel: tell the base.
  • Around the propeller. Make sure no lines, dinghy painter or mooring rope hang in the water near the stern.
Tip

Write the engine hours in the logbook every morning. With the hours and the fuel you have added, you always know roughly how much is left in the tank.

Starting the engine

  1. Batteries on: set the battery switch to the engine battery, or to both, as the base showed you.
  2. Gear lever in neutral. On most single-lever controls you can pull out the hub or press a button to add throttle without engaging gear. For starting, leave it at idle.
  3. Turn the key or switch on the panel. Warning lights come on and a buzzer sounds: that is normal before the engine runs.
  4. If the engine has a preheat button for the glow plugs, hold it for 10 to 15 seconds, then press start. Let go as soon as the engine runs, and never crank for more than about 10 seconds at a time.
  5. Once it runs, the oil pressure and charging lights go out and the buzzer stops.
  6. Look over the stern. Within a few seconds, water must come out of the exhaust in spurts with the exhaust gas. No water: stop the engine.

If it does not start after two or three tries, stop and think before you flatten the battery. Is the fuel valve open? Is the lever really in neutral? Is the stop handle pushed fully back in? Cranking again and again without a start can also fill the exhaust with seawater, which can then flood the engine.

While the engine runs

Let it warm up for a few minutes at low revs before you put load on it. Then check, and check again every hour or so while motoring:

  • Exhaust water. Still coming out in steady spurts.
  • Temperature. If there is a gauge, it usually settles around 80 to 85 °C. A needle that keeps climbing is the first sign of a cooling problem.
  • Revs. Cruise at about two thirds of the maximum, often 2000 to 2500 rpm. Faster burns much more fuel for little extra speed.
  • Smoke. A little grey smoke when you start is normal. Black smoke means the engine is overloaded: too many revs, a fouled propeller or a blocked air filter. Blue smoke means it is burning oil. White steam with little exhaust water means it is running hot.
  • Sounds and smells. A new knock, a squealing belt or a hot smell are worth a look in the engine space.

Alarms and what to do

The engine panel has warning lights and a buzzer. Treat any alarm as real until you have checked.

SignalLikely causeWhat to do
Temperature alarm, or no water from the exhaustSeacock closed, strainer blocked, broken impellerNeutral, then stop the engine when the boat is safe. Check the seacock and strainer. The impeller (the rubber vane wheel that pumps seawater) may need changing: ask the base where the spare is.
Oil pressure alarmLow oil level or an oil leakStop the engine at once and check the oil. Do not run it again until you know why.
Charging light onLoose or broken belt, alternator faultCheck the belt. You can keep motoring, but the batteries are not charging.
Engine slows down and stopsNo fuel, blocked fuel filter, air in the fuel, rope round the propellerCheck the fuel level and the filter bowl. If a rope is round the propeller, do not restart in gear: call the base.

Watch out

If the engine fails close to danger, keep the boat safe first: sail clear, or let the anchor go. Look for the fault once you are in open water. Keep the anchor ready to drop whenever you motor in or out of a harbour.

Fuel and batteries

Fuel gauges on yachts are often wrong, so plan with engine hours instead. A charter diesel burns roughly 2 to 4 litres an hour at cruising revs, and tanks hold about 100 to 200 litres. Ask the base for your boat's figures, and plan each passage so you would still arrive with at least a quarter of the tank. Fill up whenever a fuel dock is on your way.

Diesel goes into the deck filler marked DIESEL or FUEL, never the WATER filler next to it. Close the cap tightly, so rain and spray stay out.

Most yachts have an engine battery and a separate service battery for lights, fridge and instruments. The engine charges both while it runs. Never turn the battery switch off with the engine running: it can destroy the alternator. At anchor, keep an eye on the fridge and lights so the service battery is not flat by morning. The engine battery stays separate, so you can still start.

Stopping the engine

  1. Put the lever in neutral and let the engine idle for a minute or two to cool down, especially after a long spell of motoring.
  2. Stop it with the stop button, the stop handle or the key, as on your boat. A stop handle goes back in once the engine has stopped.
  3. Switch the panel off. Until you do, the buzzer sounds and the battery drains.
  4. Look in the engine space for leaks and write the engine hours in the logbook.

Practise it on the water

On our courses each crew member does the morning engine checks at least once, with the instructor showing where everything is on that boat: dipstick, strainer, belt, fuel filter and the spare impeller. You also practise what to do when the engine stops: sailing clear, anchoring and finding the fault.
