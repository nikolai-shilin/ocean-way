Making a pilotage plan

Pilotage is navigating close to land by eye: picking out marks, bearings and depths and steering between dangers, with no time to plot fixes on the chart. It starts where the passage plan ends, usually half a mile to a mile outside the harbour or anchorage, and it needs its own plan, made before you get there.

  • Pilot book. Read the entry for the harbour: approach dangers, which winds make it uncomfortable, where visitors berth and how deep it is.
  • Chart. Use the largest-scale chart or harbour plan you have. Note every rock, shoal and unlit mark near the approach, and the charted lights.
  • Plotter. Zoom in on the same area and compare it with the paper chart. Differences are a warning that one of them is out of date.
  • Satellite photos. In small Greek harbours a recent satellite image often shows a new breakwater or quay that the charts do not.

From these, decide your line of approach, what you expect to see along it, and the point where you would turn back.

Transits and leading lines

A transit is two fixed objects in line, such as a church and the end of a breakwater. While they stay in line, you are on the line through them, with no compass needed. The position fixing article explains transits as a fix. In pilotage you use them to steer.

Many harbours have leading marks or leading lights built for this: a lower front mark and a higher rear mark, set on a safe line through the approach. Steer so they stay one above the other. The rear mark shows which side you have drifted to. If it appears to the right of the front mark, you are to the right of the line, so steer to port until they line up again.

Where no leading marks exist, make your own from the chart: a headland behind a beacon, a chapel over a gap in the hills. A stern transit, two objects in line behind you, works just as well when you leave.

Clearing bearings

A clearing bearing marks the edge of safe water. You draw a line from a charted object past the danger, and read off its bearing. Write it as "not more than" or "not less than", for example: the lighthouse must bear not less than 040°.

Check it with a hand-bearing compass, or with the steering compass if you point the bow at the object. As long as the bearing stays on the safe side, you are clear of the rock, wherever you are along the line. Hatch the danger side of the line on the chart so the meaning is obvious at a glance.

Depth contours as a handrail

The depth sounder gives you a second, independent check. Pick a depth contour on the chart that keeps you clear of the dangers, and follow it like a handrail along a dark staircase. If the depth drops below that figure, turn towards deeper water at once.

Charter yachts of 11 to 14 metres draw about 1.8 to 2.2 metres. Choose a contour with a margin, often 5 or 10 metres, and know whether your sounder shows depth below the keel, below the transducer or below the surface. With almost no tide in Greece, the charted depth is close to what you will find, but old surveys can miss a rock.

Waypoints and the plotter close in

The plotter is excellent in open water and much less reliable in the last few hundred metres. Many Greek harbours were charted from old surveys, and the electronic chart can be offset by tens of metres. The boat may appear on the quay while you are still in the channel. Zoomed in, the screen can also hide a rock that only appears at another scale.

  • Put your last waypoint in clear, deep water outside the entrance, never on the entrance itself.
  • From that waypoint on, steer by eye, transits and depth, and use the plotter as a check.
  • Never let the autopilot follow a route into a harbour.

GPS can also be jammed. Interference has been reported repeatedly in parts of the eastern Mediterranean, so be ready to pilot without it.

A pilotage notebook and sketch

At the helm you cannot study a chart. Write the plan on a single page, in large letters, and keep it in a clear plastic sleeve where the helm can see it.

  • Each leg: course, distance and the transit or bearing that marks it.
  • What you should see: lights, colours, buildings, the shape of the land.
  • The safe depth, and the clearing bearings for every danger.
  • Where to turn, and where you would turn back.

Add a simple sketch of the harbour: the entrance, the breakwater lights, the visitors' quay, the ferry berth to keep clear of and the depths. A rough drawing you made yourself is easier to read in a hurry than any chart.

Preparing the boat and crew

Finish the work outside, in open water, so nobody is busy on deck in the entrance.

  • Sails. Furl the genoa and drop or furl the main before you start the approach.
  • Fenders and lines. Rig fenders on both sides at a middle height until you know your berth, and have bow and stern lines ready.
  • Anchor. Ready to drop: chain lock or lashing off, windlass switched on, someone at the bow who knows how to let it go. If the engine fails in a narrow entrance, the anchor stops you drifting onto the rocks.
  • Jobs. One person on the helm, one on lookout at the bow, one reading the depth and the notes. Tell everyone the plan and the turn-back point.

Timing: daylight and wind

Plan to arrive in daylight, ideally by mid-afternoon. In summer the afternoon breeze, or the meltemi (the strong summer northerly) further out in the Aegean, makes a crosswind berth much harder, and popular Saronic harbours such as Hydra and Poros are often full by then.

Check how the forecast wind meets the entrance. A harbour open to the wind can have swell breaking across its mouth, and a quay facing the wind is a poor berth. Ferries and fast hydrofoils use the main harbours of the Saronic islands: keep clear of their berths and listen for their horns.

When it doesn't look as expected

Sooner or later the view will not match the plan: the light is not where it should be, the depth is less than charted, or there are two possible gaps in the rocks. That is the moment to slow down, not to press on.

  1. Slow down or stop in water you know is safe.
  2. If in doubt, turn back along the line you came in on.
  3. Hold off in deep water, motoring slowly in circles or hove-to.
  4. Fix your position from bearings and depth, and identify each mark again.
  5. Go in only once everything agrees. If it never does, choose another harbour.
Watch out

A common mistake is to enter the wrong bay, one that looks like the right one from seaward. Check your position before every entry, not only when something feels wrong.

Entering at night

Many charter agreements do not allow night sailing, and entering a strange harbour in the dark is best avoided. If you have to, plan for it in daylight.

  • Use only lit marks, and time each light to be sure which one it is, as the buoyage article explains.
  • Look for a sector light: a white sector shows the safe line, with red or green either side.
  • Town lights hide navigation lights and make distances hard to judge. Unlit rocks, moored boats and mooring buoys stay invisible until you are close.
  • Go slowly, follow your depth contour and use a red light at the chart so your eyes stay adjusted.
Tip

Often the safer choice is to wait outside in open water, hove-to or anchored in a sheltered bay nearby, and go in at first light.
