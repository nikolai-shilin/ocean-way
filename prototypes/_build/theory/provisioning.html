Planning meals for the week

On a week's charter in Greece most crews eat breakfast and lunch on board, lunch usually at anchor after a swim. Dinner is often ashore, because most harbours and many anchorages have a taverna. Plan to cook dinner on board on two or three nights out of seven, and keep one simple dinner in reserve in case the weather keeps you in a bay with no taverna.

  • Breakfast. Coffee and tea, yoghurt with honey, fruit and bread.
  • Lunch. Salads, bread, feta, olives, cold meats and fruit, with little or no cooking.
  • Dinners aboard. One-pot meals that need only one burner, such as pasta with a sauce, rice or a stew.
  • Under way. Snacks you can eat with one hand, such as nuts and biscuits.

Do one big shop on the first day for the basics and two or three days of fresh food. After that, buy fresh food every two or three days in harbour, which saves fridge space.

Buy the basics once for the whole boat: coffee, olive oil, salt, tinned food for the reserve dinner, washing-up liquid, toilet paper and strong bin bags. Ask the base what is already on board first.

Tip

Leave cardboard boxes ashore. Cardboard takes up space, soaks up damp and can bring cockroach eggs on board.

Drinking water and the water tanks

Drinking water. Drink bottled water, because the quality of the tank water varies from harbour to harbour. Allow 2 to 3 litres per person per day in summer, and 4 litres or more on a hot, still day. For a crew of six for a week that is about 100 to 130 litres, or 11 to 15 packs of six 1.5-litre bottles.

Water tanks. A charter monohull usually carries 200 to 400 litres of fresh water, and a catamaran often more. A crew of six with long showers can empty that in two days. With care, the same water lasts four or five days.

  • Showers. Swim first, then rinse off with the transom shower (the hose at the stern). Wet yourself, turn the water off, soap, then rinse: that uses 5 to 10 litres.
  • Washing up. In a clean anchorage, wash up in a bowl of seawater and rinse with a little fresh water. Never use seawater from inside a harbour.
  • The pump. Switch the water pump off at the panel at night and when everyone goes ashore. If it keeps running and no water comes out, the tank is empty: switch it off before it is damaged.
  • Filling up. Fill the tanks in every harbour that has water, often paid in cash. Use the deck filler marked WATER, never the one marked FUEL or DIESEL.

The fridge and ice

A charter monohull usually has a fridge of around 100 litres, often top-opening. A catamaran usually has a larger one.

  • Power. The fridge is the biggest user of the service battery. At anchor, run the engine for about an hour morning and evening, or use shore power in harbour, as the base advises.
  • Keep it shut. Decide what you need, take it all out at once and close the lid. Every opening lets cold air out.
  • Load it cold and full. Buy food and drinks already chilled where you can. A full fridge stays cold longer, so fill the gaps with bottles of water.
  • Coldest at the bottom. Put meat and fish at the bottom in a sealed box, and keep only one day's drinks in the fridge.

Ice. Bags of ice are sold in most harbours. A bag in the fridge or a cool box helps on hot days. Leave the ice in its bag so the melt water does not soak the food.

Stowing food so it stays put

When the boat heels (leans over under sail) or rolls in a swell, anything loose slides, falls or flies across the cabin. Before you leave, stow everything as if the boat were about to lean over by 30 degrees.

  • Heavy things low. Water, cans and bottles go in the lowest lockers, near the middle of the boat.
  • Fruit and vegetables. Keep them in a net or an open crate in a shaded locker, and throw out anything going soft.
  • Lockers. Close every locker with its catch. A locker on the low side can burst open when you change tack.
  • Clear surfaces. Nothing stays on the worktop, the saloon table or the chart table under way.

Lockers and personal kit

Each cabin has a hanging locker and a few shelves, and that is roughly what each person gets. Bring your kit in a soft bag, such as a holdall, which folds flat under a mattress once it is empty. A hard suitcase does not fit in a locker and takes up a bunk all week.

Pack light: shorts, T-shirts, swimwear, a warm layer for the evenings and a light waterproof jacket. Add soft-soled shoes that stay on board, a hat, sun cream, a head torch and any medicines you need. Peg wet towels and swimwear on the guardrails (the wires round the deck edge), not on the bunks.

Cooking at sea safely

Cooking is safest in harbour or at anchor. For cooking under way, the galley (the boat's kitchen) has some help built in:

  • Gimballed stove. The stove hangs on pivots called gimbals, so it swings and stays level when the boat heels.
  • Fiddles and pan clamps. Fiddles are raised rails round the stove top that stop pans sliding off. Clamps hold each pan on its burner.
  • Crash bar. This strong rail in front of the stove stops you falling onto it and gives you something to hold.

In a rough sea, do not boil pans of water under way. Scalds are a common injury on yachts. Fill a vacuum flask with hot water and make lunch before you leave instead. If you must heat something, fill the pan no more than half full and wear long trousers.

Gas safety is covered in the safety on board guide in this series. In short, turn the gas off at the bottle after every meal and keep the fire blanket within reach of the cook.

Shops, rubbish and cash ashore

Delivery to the boat. Around the big Athens marinas, such as Alimos and Lavrio, several supermarkets take an order by phone or online and deliver it to the boat. Order a day or two ahead, and you can leave on the first morning. Small islands have a mini market and a bakery, but less choice and higher prices.

Rubbish. Nothing goes over the side, not even food scraps. Keep the bin bag tied in a cockpit locker and take it ashore to the bins on or near the quay. Greek blue bins are for recycling, and plastic, glass, cans and paper go in together. On most charter yachts, used toilet paper goes in the small bin by the toilet, because the marine toilet blocks easily.

Cash. Cards are taken in most places around Athens and on the larger islands. On small islands, card machines sometimes fail, harbour fees and water are often paid in cash, and the only cash machine can run empty in August. Carry enough cash for two or three days.

Practise it on the water

On our courses the crew plans the meals and does the first shop together, and you learn to stow the boat so nothing moves when she heels. During the week you keep track of the water and the fridge, fill the tanks in harbour and cook at anchor, using the stove and the gas safely.
