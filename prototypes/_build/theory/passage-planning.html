Four stages of a passage plan

A passage plan is thinking the day through before you leave, so nothing comes as a surprise. Professional mariners use four stages, and they work just as well on a charter yacht:

  1. Gather information. Charts, pilot book, forecasts, harbour details and anything that has changed recently.
  2. Plan. Choose the route, the waypoints, the timing and the alternatives.
  3. Execute. Brief the crew, prepare the boat and sail the plan.
  4. Monitor. Check your progress against the plan all the way, and change it when things change.

Many sailing books also teach a checklist under the memory word APPROACH. It covers the same ground: alternative harbours, pilotage into and out of port, dangers on the way, tides and streams, charts, communications and daylight. The exact words vary between books. What matters is that nothing is left out.

Gather the information

  • Charts. A small-scale chart for the whole passage and large-scale charts, or harbour plans, for the departure, the destination and any tricky stretch. Use paper and electronic charts together.
  • Pilot book. The sailing guide for the area. It describes harbours and anchorages, which winds they are open to, approach dangers, and where to find fuel and water.
  • Almanac and notices. Lights, radio channels, tide tables where they matter, and Notices to Mariners (official updates such as a new wreck, a moved buoy or a light that is out). NAVTEX and coast radio broadcast navigational warnings too.
  • Forecasts. At least two sources, for today and the next couple of days, so you know whether you can get back or move on.

Route, waypoints and dangers

Draw the route on the chart, then measure each leg on the latitude scale. Keep well off headlands, shoals and lee shores (a coast the wind is blowing onto). The shortest line is rarely the best.

Waypoints. A waypoint is a position you enter into the plotter to steer towards. Put them in clear water, never on a rock, buoy or headland, where other boats steer for the same mark. Check every leg on the largest-scale chart.

Clearing bearings. A clearing bearing is a compass bearing on a charted object that marks the edge of safe water. For example, if a reef lies off a point, you might note that you are safe while a lighthouse bears not more than 080°. Draw the line on the chart, write the bearing in your plan and check it with a hand-bearing compass.

Safe depth contour. Pick a depth contour, often 10 or 20 metres, that keeps you clear of the dangers along a coast. If the echo sounder shows less than that, you are too close, and you head out.

Time, daylight and the afternoon wind

Estimate the time by dividing the distance by a realistic average speed. For a charter yacht, 5 to 6 knots is a fair figure, less if you are beating (sailing towards the wind) or the sea is rough. Add time for leaving and entering harbour.

  • Daylight. Check sunrise and sunset, and plan to arrive with at least an hour of light to spare. Many charter agreements do not allow sailing at night, and a strange anchorage is much harder to enter in the dark.
  • Afternoon wind. In summer, the sea breeze and the meltemi usually build through the day and peak in the afternoon. Leave early so the exposed stretches are behind you before the wind fills in, especially if it will be on the nose.
  • Tidal gates. In tidal waters, some headlands and channels can only be passed sensibly at certain states of tide, when the stream is slack or running your way. Portland Bill on the English Channel is a classic example. Work back from the gate to find your departure time. On our home waters a channel current or the wind accelerating round a cape can play the same role.

Bolt holes and plan B

A bolt hole is an alternative harbour or anchorage you can run to if the weather turns, the crew is unwell or something breaks. Choose at least one for each part of the passage, and note which winds it shelters from.

Set decision points too. For example: if we are not past the headland by 1100, we turn back. Deciding in advance, while everyone is calm, is far easier than deciding on a pitching boat with a tired crew.

Worked example: Kaş to Kekova

A summer day leg on the Lycian coast of Turkey, from Kaş marina east to an anchorage near Üçağız, inside the sheltered water behind Kekova Island. The total is about 16 miles. The waypoints below are illustrative, so plot your own from the chart.

TotalAbout 16 milesAbout 3 hours 15 minutesLeave 0800, at anchor by about 1130

In a typical summer pattern on this coast, the afternoon wind blows from the west, so the leg runs with it behind you. Leaving at 0800 gets you in before it peaks, with time to take a shore line. If a strong southerly is forecast, the open coast is uncomfortable, and you would wait in Kaş.

Prepare the boat and brief the crew

Check fuel and water before you go, enough for the passage and a good reserve, and note where you can refill. Check the engine, bilges and safety gear, and stow anything loose.

Then brief the crew. Tell them the route, the timing, the expected weather, where the bolt holes are and who does what. Show them the life jackets, radio and first aid kit.

Write the plan down: waypoints, courses, distances, times, clearing bearings, bolt holes and key radio channels. Leave a copy, or at least your route and expected arrival time, with someone ashore, such as the charter base or a friend, and tell them when to raise the alarm.

Monitoring under way

A plan is only useful if you check against it. Write up the log every hour and at each waypoint: time, log reading, course, speed, wind, barometer and position. Compare where you are with where the plan says you should be.

  • Are you ahead or behind? Work out a new arrival time.
  • Is the wind doing what the forecast said? If not, rethink the next leg.
  • Is the crew coping? Fatigue and seasickness are good reasons to use a bolt hole.
Watch out

The commonest mistake is to stick to the plan after it has stopped making sense. If you reach a decision point and the conditions are worse than expected, take the alternative. Changing the plan is good seamanship, not a failure.

Practise it on the water

On our courses you plan each day's passage yourself the evening before, from the chart, the pilot book and the forecast. In the morning you brief the crew, then skipper the passage, keep the log every hour and decide when to change the plan, with an instructor beside you.
