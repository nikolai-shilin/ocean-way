The calls and what they mean

The helm (the person steering) leads every tack and gybe with a short set of calls, so the whole crew knows what is about to happen. Some skippers use slightly different words, so listen to the briefing.

CallWhen it is saidWhat it means
"Ready about"Before a tackGet into position and prepare the sheets.
"Lee-oh" or "Helm's a-lee"As the helm starts to turnThe bow is turning through the wind now.
"Ready to gybe"Before a gybePrepare the sheets and centre the mainsail.
"Gybe-oh"As the stern turns through the windThe boom is coming across now.

After the first call, each crew member answers "Ready" once in place, and the helm waits for everyone. Before calling, the helm looks all round, including behind the headsail.

Who does what

  • On the helm. Gives the calls, keeps the lookout and steers through the turn at a steady rate.
  • On the old sheet. Releases the working sheet, the headsail sheet holding the sail now, on the leeward side (away from the wind).
  • On the new sheet. Pulls in the lazy sheet, the unused one on the windward side, as the sail comes across.
  • On the mainsheet. Handles the mainsheet and the traveller (the sliding car it is attached to). This job matters most in a gybe.

With a small crew, one person covers several jobs. Everyone else stays clear of the sheets and below the path of the boom.

How to tack, step by step

A tack takes you from close-hauled on one tack to close-hauled on the other, turning the bow through about 90° on most cruising yachts. Start with good speed, so the boat carries her way through the wind.

  1. The helm checks all round and calls "Ready about".
  2. Put two or three turns of the new sheet clockwise round the windward winch, pull out the slack and keep the handle within reach.
  3. Take the old sheet out of the self-tailer (the jaws on top of the winch) or off its cleat, keeping the turns on and the tail in your hand. Answer "Ready".
  4. The helm calls "Lee-oh" and turns steadily towards the wind.
  5. As the headsail starts to back (fill from the wrong side), release the old sheet. Lift the tail so the turns spin off the drum.
  6. Pull the new sheet in hand over hand while the sail crosses, before the wind loads it, then winch in the last part.

Do not over-steer. Stop turning on the new close-hauled course. If you carry on onto a reach, the headsail fills hard before it is in, and the crew cannot winch the last part. Turning too slowly is the opposite fault: the boat stops head to wind.

Winches without riding turns

A riding turn is when the loaded part of the sheet climbs over the turns on the winch and jams them.

  • Wrap clockwise. Almost all yacht winches turn clockwise.
  • Keep tension on the tail. Slack turns under load are the usual cause. If you need both hands for the handle, use the self-tailer.
  • Add turns as the load builds. Two or three to pull in slack fast, three or four under full load.
  • Check the lead. The sheet should come onto the drum from slightly below.
Watch out

Keep fingers clear of the drum and of the point where the sheet goes onto it. To ease a loaded sheet, hold the turns with the flat of your hand and let them slip. If you get a riding turn, stop winching and tell the skipper: the usual fix is to take the load off with another line.

If the tack stalls: in irons

Sometimes the boat stops head to wind, sails flapping, with no steering. This is being in irons. It usually follows a slow approach, a slow turn or a short chop that stops the bow.

  1. Hold the headsail out on the side opposite to where you want the bow to go. The wind backs it and pushes the bow away.
  2. Ease the mainsheet, so the mainsail does not push the bow back into the wind.
  3. As the boat drifts backwards, the steering works in reverse: turn the wheel the opposite way to where you want the bow to go.
  4. Once on a close reach, sheet the headsail in on the leeward side, centre the wheel and build speed.

If you start the engine to help, first check that no lines are trailing in the water.

How to gybe under control

A controlled gybe keeps the swing of the boom short by bringing it to the centre first.

  1. From a broad reach, the helm checks all round and calls "Ready to gybe".
  2. Release the preventer (a line holding the boom forward) if one is rigged. Gybing against it can break the boom.
  3. The helm bears away slowly to a run while the mainsheet is pulled in until the boom is near the centreline. Everyone keeps their head low and answers "Ready".
  4. The helm calls "Gybe-oh" and turns the stern gently through the wind. The boom crosses a short distance.
  5. Ease the mainsheet out steadily on the new side, with turns kept on the winch. The helm meets the boat with a little opposite wheel, as she tends to round up (turn sharply towards the wind).
  6. Release the headsail on the old side and sheet it in on the new one.

Keep the kicker (the vang, which holds the boom down) on throughout, so the boom cannot lift.

Why a gybe needs more care

What changesIn a tackIn a gybe
Passes through the windThe bowThe stern
Boom movementA short way, sail emptyRight across, sail full
Boat speedDrops, can stallKept up
Main riskGetting stuck in ironsInjury or rig damage from the boom

In a tack the sails empty as the bow passes through the wind. In a gybe they stay full, so the load hits the boom at once. The worst case is the accidental gybe described in our points of sail guide. In a strong breeze, many skippers tack round instead, turning the long way.

Tacking and gybing a catamaran

  • Tacks are slower. A catamaran is light, with no heavy keel to carry her through the wind, so she loses speed fast and stalls easily in a chop.
  • Keep the speed up. Sail a little freer than close-hauled to build speed, then turn firmly. Let the headsail back a moment longer to push the bows round.
  • Use an engine if needed. In light wind and waves, a short burst on one engine helps the bows through.
  • Gybes feel calmer, but the boom is big. She hardly rolls or heels, but the mainsail is large and the traveller runs on a wide track. Centre both before you turn.

Some catamarans have a self-tacking jib, which crosses on its own track with no sheet work.
