Chart basics

Every position on a chart is given by two numbers. Latitude is how far north or south of the equator you are, from 0° to 90°. Longitude is how far east or west of Greenwich, from 0° to 180°. Each degree is divided into 60 minutes. Most of the Mediterranean lies between about 30° and 46° North.

One minute of latitude equals one nautical mile (1,852 metres). So to measure distance, use the latitude scale on the left or right side of the chart, level with where you are measuring. Never use the longitude scale along the top and bottom: a minute of longitude gets shorter as you move away from the equator.

Chart datum is the low-water level the printed depths are measured from. With very little tide in most of the Mediterranean, depths on the chart are close to what you will find. Also check the note on which horizontal datum the chart uses. Modern charts use WGS84, the same as GPS, but some older charts do not, and positions can then be offset.

Why paper still matters

A chartplotter is a superb tool, and you should use it. But it depends on power, a dry screen, a satellite signal and a chart that is correct. Any of these can fail. Batteries go flat. Some remote bays are charted from old surveys, and the electronic chart can be offset from reality. GPS signals can also be jammed or spoofed, and interference has been reported repeatedly in parts of the eastern Mediterranean in recent years.

A paper chart with your position marked every hour means that if the screen goes blank, you know where you are and where the dangers are. It is also the best way to understand what the plotter is telling you.

Dead reckoning

Dead reckoning (DR) is working out your position from where you were, which way you steered and how far you went. You need three things:

  • your last known position (a fix), with the time;
  • the course you steered, converted to true;
  • the distance run, from the log (the instrument that measures distance through the water).

Example: at 1000 you fixed your position off Hydra, the log read 12.4 and you steered 090°T. At 1100 the log reads 18.4, so you have run 6 miles. Draw a line from the fix along 090°, measure 6 miles on the latitude scale, and mark it with the time. That is your DR position.

Estimated position

A DR ignores everything that pushes the boat sideways. An estimated position (EP) adds it back. Mark it with a small triangle and the time.

Leeway is the sideways slip caused by the wind. Close-hauled (sailing as close to the wind as you can) in a fresh breeze, a cruising yacht may make 5–10° of leeway. With the wind behind, it is close to zero. Watch the angle between your wake and the centreline to judge it. Apply it away from the wind: if the wind is on the port (left) side, add leeway to your heading; on the starboard (right) side, subtract it.

Current. Tidal streams are weak in most of the Mediterranean, but water still moves. Currents run through straits and channels, and a meltemi blowing for several days sets up a surface drift. They are often under a knot, but stronger in some places. Check the pilot book. If you know the set (direction) and rate, draw it from your water track for the time elapsed.

The three-point bearing fix

A fix is a position you have measured, not estimated. The classic method uses a hand-bearing compass, a small compass you hold to your eye.

  1. Pick three objects that are on the chart and easy to identify: a lighthouse, a church, the end of a headland. They should be well spread, ideally 60° or more apart.
  2. Take the bearings quickly. Take objects ahead or astern first and those on the beam (off the side) last, because beam bearings change fastest.
  3. Note the time and the log reading.
  4. Convert each bearing to true by applying variation.
  5. Draw each line on the chart towards the object.

The three lines almost never meet at a point. They form a small triangle called the cocked hat. A small cocked hat means a good fix. A large one means a mistake: a wrongly identified object or a misread bearing. Take the bearings again. For safety, assume you are at the corner of the triangle nearest to danger.

Transits and depth contours

A transit is two charted objects in line, such as a beacon and a chapel. When they line up, you are exactly on the line through them. No compass is needed, so there is no compass error. Many harbours have leading marks or lights built for this. A transit is also a quick way to check your steering compass.

Depth is a useful cross-check. If your fix puts you on the 20-metre contour but the echo sounder shows 45 metres, something is wrong. Know whether your sounder shows depth below the transducer, below the keel or below the surface.

Variation and deviation

Charts are drawn to true north. Your compass points to magnetic north, and it is also pulled aside by the boat's own metal and electrics. So you correct in two steps:

  • Variation is the difference between true and magnetic north. It depends on place and year, and it is printed on the compass rose of the chart. In the Aegean it is small and east, around 5°E, and changing slowly.
  • Deviation is the error caused by the boat itself. It changes with the boat's heading and is shown on the boat's deviation card.

Remember the order: True, Variation, Magnetic, Deviation, Compass ("True Virgins Make Dull Companions"). Going from true to compass, add west and subtract east.

Worked example: you want to steer 045°T. Variation is 5°E, so subtract: 040°M. The deviation card says 3°W on this heading, so add: 043°C. You steer 043 on the compass. Going the other way, from compass to true, you reverse the signs.

Keep a logbook every hour

At sea, write up the log every hour, and at every change of course or sail. Record the time, log reading, course steered, speed, wind direction and force, barometer, and your position with how you got it (GPS, fix or EP). Note events too: reefing, engine on, sightings.

If everything electronic fails, your last log entry is the start point for a new DR. It is also the boat's official record of the passage.

Practise it on the water

On our courses you plot DR and EP on paper alongside the chartplotter, take three-point fixes along the coast and compare them with the GPS. Each hour you write up the log, so the routine stays with you after the course.
