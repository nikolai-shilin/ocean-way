Batteries and the battery switch

A charter yacht runs on 12 volts (12 V), like a car. Most have two separate sets of batteries:

  • Engine battery. One battery that only starts the engine, so you can still start it when everything else is flat.
  • Service bank. Two to four batteries that run the lights, fridge, water pump, instruments and autopilot. On a yacht of 11 to 14 metres it usually holds 200 to 400 amp hours (Ah), a measure of how much charge it stores.

The battery switches are usually near the chart table or the companionway steps: either one switch for each bank, or a rotary switch marked 1, 2, BOTH and OFF. Ask at the base briefing which position to use.

The 12 V panel at the chart table has a breaker (a switch that also protects the circuit) for each item. Turn on only what you need. The 230 V sockets work only on shore power or through an inverter (a box that makes 230 V from the batteries).

Watch out

Never turn the battery switch to OFF while the engine is running. It can destroy the alternator in seconds.

Reading the voltage and charging

Most charter yachts have lead-acid or AGM batteries (a sealed type of lead-acid battery). The panel shows the voltage of each bank. It tells you how full the battery is only at rest, with no charging and almost nothing switched on for at least half an hour, for example early in the morning.

Voltage at restState of chargeWhat to do
12.7 V or moreFully chargedNo action needed
12.4 VAbout three quartersFine for the day
12.2 VAbout halfCharge soon
12.0 VAbout a quarterCharge now and switch off what you can
Below 11.8 VEmptyThe batteries are being damaged. Charge at once

Try not to take a lead-acid bank below about half charge, because each deep discharge shortens its life. While charging, the panel shows 13.5 to 14.4 V, which tells you only that charging is working.

  • From the engine. The alternator charges both banks whenever the engine runs. At anchor, an hour at about 1500 rpm in neutral, morning and evening, usually keeps the bank topped up. Do not run it late in the evening in a quiet anchorage.
  • Shore power. In a marina or on a town quay with power points, connect the cable to the boat first and to the quay last, and disconnect the quay end first. Then switch on the battery charger.
  • Solar panels. Many charter yachts now have panels on the bimini (the cockpit sun cover). On a sunny Greek summer day, 300 watts of panels can put back roughly 60 to 90 Ah, which covers much of the fridge.

The fridge is the biggest user. Open it briefly, keep it full and put drinks in already cool. The anchor windlass draws over 100 A, so run the engine while you use it.

Fresh water

A yacht of 11 to 14 metres carries about 300 to 500 litres of fresh water, often in two or three tanks. A careful crew uses 20 to 30 litres per person a day, so with six people it lasts only two or three days.

  • The pump. An electric pump keeps the water under pressure, so the taps work like at home. Switch it on at the panel. If you hear the pump running with every tap closed, the tank is empty or a pipe is leaking: switch it off and find out why.
  • Changing tanks. You often choose the tank with a valve under a bunk. The gauges are often wrong, so note when you filled each tank.
  • Hot water. The hot water tank, the calorifier, is heated by the engine and by shore power.
  • Filling up. Fill through the deck filler marked WATER, never the one marked DIESEL or FUEL. In Greek harbours water is often sold by the port attendant or from a meter on the quay. On small islands such as Hydra it can be scarce.

Drink bottled water and use the tank water for washing and cooking. Wash dishes in seawater first and rinse them in fresh water. A watermaker, which makes fresh water from seawater, is rare on charter yachts.

Gas for the cooker

The cooker runs on bottled gas (LPG, usually butane in Greece). The bottle sits in a gas locker in the cockpit or on deck, which drains overboard. A full bottle lasts a week or more of normal cooking.

Between the bottle and the cooker there is usually a solenoid valve, an electric valve switched from a panel in the galley (kitchen), or a manual valve near the cooker. To cook:

  1. Open the valve on the bottle, if it is closed, and switch on the solenoid or open the galley valve.
  2. Hold the flame to the burner, then push in and turn the knob.
  3. Keep the knob pressed for about ten seconds. A safety device cuts the gas if the flame goes out, and it needs that time to warm up.

After cooking, turn off the solenoid or the valve first and let the flame burn the gas left in the pipe. Then turn off the knob.

Watch out

LPG is heavier than air. A leak sinks into the bilge, collects there and can explode with a single spark. Sniff around the cooker and the bilge every day. If you smell gas, turn it off at the bottle, use no flames or switches, open the hatches and pump the bilge with the manual pump.

Changing a bottle. Close every knob and the bottle valve, with no flames nearby. Move the regulator to the new bottle, checking that its rubber seal is in place. Open the valve and brush soapy water on the joint: bubbles mean a leak.

The heads and the holding tank

The heads (marine toilet) pumps seawater in and waste out through seacocks (valves on holes through the hull). Most charter yachts have a hand pump with a small lever beside it:

  1. Set the lever to flush, the setting that lets seawater in, and pump 10 to 15 full strokes until the bowl and the pipe are clear.
  2. Set the lever to dry and pump until the bowl is empty.
  3. Leave it on dry, so seawater cannot siphon in and flood the boat when she heels.

Put nothing in the toilet that you have not eaten first: wet wipes and sanitary products block it. Many charter bases ask you to put toilet paper in the bin as well, as is normal ashore in Greece. A blocked toilet is often charged to the deposit.

The waste goes into a holding tank, or overboard, depending on a valve the base will show you. Use the holding tank in harbours, anchorages and near beaches. Empty it only at sea, well away from the coast, by opening its seacock. Three miles from land is a common rule, and the base will tell you what applies locally. A tank of 50 to 80 litres fills in a day or two with a full crew, and a smell from its vent on deck means it is nearly full.

Bilge pumps and seacocks

Most yachts have an automatic electric bilge pump and a manual pump worked from the cockpit. Look in the bilge every morning, and if the electric pump runs often, find out why. Taste the water: fresh water means a leaking tank or pipe, and salt water means a leak from the sea. The shower usually drains into a small sump with its own pump, which you switch on after each shower.

A seacock is open when its lever is in line with the pipe and closed when the lever is across it. Learn where each one is on the first day, and close the heads seacocks when you leave the boat.

Practise it on the water

On our courses you find every battery switch, tank, valve and seacock on the first day, and read the battery voltage each morning. You change tanks, pump the heads, empty the holding tank at sea and plan the water, power and gas for a week at anchor.
