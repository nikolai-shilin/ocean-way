What being the skipper means

On a bareboat charter there is no professional skipper on board. The person named as skipper on the charter contract and the crew list holds the licence, and in law that person is responsible for the yacht and everyone on board. In Greece the port police (the coastguard) check the papers with the skipper, and after an accident they speak to the skipper first.

This responsibility covers safe navigation under the COLREGs, the safety briefing, local harbour and anchoring rules, and the condition of the boat. International law also obliges you to help anyone in distress at sea, as far as you can without putting your own crew in danger.

In practice, you make the final decisions and the crew can rely on them. You do not have to do every job yourself: a skipper who stays off the helm during a manoeuvre often sees more.

Choosing the crew

A 12-metre yacht is comfortable for four to six people. Before you book, ask each person about their experience, swimming, seasickness and any medical condition.

Choose one person as your mate (second in command). In Greece the charter company usually asks for a second crew member with sailing experience as well as the licensed skipper. On the first day, show your mate how to start and stop the engine, send a Mayday on the VHF, recover a person from the water and drop the anchor. If the skipper is the one who falls ill or goes overboard, the mate brings the boat home.

Briefings that work

The safety on board guide in this series lists what a safety briefing covers. What makes it work is how you give it:

  • Everyone, on board, before you leave. Give it in harbour, with the whole crew present, rested and sober.
  • Show and touch. Each person fits their own lifejacket, finds the fire extinguisher and switches on the VHF radio.
  • Keep it short. People remember little after about fifteen minutes.
  • Check by asking. Instead of asking "Any questions?", ask "Where is the fire blanket?" or "How do you turn off the gas?"
  • Repeat it. Brief anyone who joins later, and go over the key points again on the second morning.

A job for everyone in a manoeuvre

Before every manoeuvre, in open water, tell each person what they will do, where they will stand and what to do if it goes wrong. For a stern-to berth with five people, the jobs might be:

JobWhereWhat they do
HelmAt the wheelSteers and works the engine, following the skipper's calls.
BowAt the windlassDrops and lifts the anchor, or picks up the lazy line, and signals to the helm.
Stern linesOne each side at the sternHands or throws the lines ashore and makes them fast.
FendersMoving along the side deckHolds a spare fender where the boat may touch another.

Keep the same jobs for the first day or two, then rotate so everyone learns every role. Anyone without a job sits in the cockpit. The med mooring guide in this series covers each job in detail.

Clear calls and one voice on deck

During a manoeuvre, only one person gives orders: the skipper. If someone else is on the helm, they steer and listen.

  • Name first, then the action. Say "Maria, stern line ashore now", not "Someone get that line".
  • Answer back. The person repeats the call or says "Done", so you know it has happened.
  • Point, do not say left or right. Left and right change when people face different ways, but a pointing arm does not.
  • Agree hand signals. The helm cannot hear the bow over the engine, so agree signals for stop, ahead, astern and the direction of the chain. Shouting louder does not help.

One exception: anyone may call "Stop" if they see danger, such as a hand near a winch or a swimmer near the propeller.

Planning the day and sharing the chores

Share the plan at breakfast: where you are going, how far, when you leave, when you expect to arrive and what the wind will do. A typical Saronic day is a leg of 15 to 20 miles, such as Aegina to Poros, with a swim stop on the way and arrival before the afternoon wind builds.

Where there is a safe choice, let the crew make it, such as which of two bays for lunch. People who helped make the plan work harder to keep it.

Write a rota for the chores and pin it by the chart table: cooking, washing up, cleaning the heads (toilets), filling water, taking rubbish ashore. If strong wind is forecast at anchor overnight, set an anchor watch: pairs take turns of one or two hours and wake the skipper at once if anything changes.

Decisions and saying no

Listen to the crew, then decide, and say the decision out loud. Decide early, while there is still an easy alternative. The weather guide in this series has a go or no-go checklist.

  • Weather. If the forecast worries you, stay or choose a shorter, sheltered leg. Plan to be back at the base on the evening before check-out, so a flight home never pushes you to sail in bad weather.
  • Alcohol. No alcohol for the skipper or the helm until the boat is secured for the night, and none for anyone driving the dinghy. The port police can breath-test a skipper, and many charter insurance policies do not cover an accident after drinking.
  • Tiredness. A late night ashore followed by an early start is the classic mistake. If you or the crew are tired, shorten the day.

When you say no, give the reason in a sentence and offer an alternative: "Not round the headland today: the gusts are too strong. We will swim here and go tomorrow morning." Do not argue about safety.

Looking after people

A sick or exhausted crew makes mistakes. Watch for the early signs, because people often hide them.

ProblemEarly signsWhat to do
SeasicknessYawning, going quiet, pale skin, cold sweatKeep them on deck, looking at the horizon, and let them helm. Most tablets work only if taken one to two hours before you leave.
Heat exhaustionHeadache, dizziness, nausea, heavy sweatingMove them into shade, cool their skin with water and give them small, frequent drinks.
DehydrationThirst, dark urine, headache, tirednessDrink 2 to 3 litres a day, more in hot weather. Alcohol, coffee and seasickness all make it worse.
FatigueSlow reactions, small mistakes, short tempersRest in turns, and keep helm spells to about an hour in strong sun. The skipper needs rest too.

In July and August the Saronic Gulf is often 30 to 35 °C with strong sun. Rig the bimini (the cockpit sun cover), and wear hats and sun cream of factor 30 or more.

Watch out

A seasick person must never lean over the rail alone. Keep them in the cockpit, clipped on or with someone holding them. Confusion with hot, dry skin can mean heatstroke: cool the person at once and call for medical help on VHF channel 16 or by phone on 112.

Children and nervous crew

Children. A child wears a lifejacket of the right size, with crotch straps, whenever they are on deck or in the dinghy. At any moment one named adult is responsible for each child, so nobody assumes someone else is watching. Under way, children stay in the cockpit unless an adult goes with them. Give them real jobs, such as fenders. Swimming happens only with the engine off and an adult watching from the boat.

Nervous crew. Explain what will happen before it happens, such as heeling or the noise of the anchor chain. Start in light wind and reef early. Give a nervous person the helm in calm water, because being in control usually eases fear.

The debrief

After a manoeuvre, once the boat is secure, take five minutes while everyone still remembers it. Ask three questions: what went well, what went wrong, and what will we do differently next time.

Let the crew answer first, then add your own view. Start with your own mistakes, so others feel free to admit theirs. Talk about the plan and the calls, not about who was at fault. A short debrief after each berthing makes the next one calmer.
