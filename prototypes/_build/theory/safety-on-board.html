The skipper's safety briefing

Before the yacht leaves the berth, the skipper gives the crew a safety briefing. Everyone should know the answers by the end, because the person who needs help may be the skipper. A good briefing covers:

  • Lifejackets and harnesses: where they are, how to fit them and when to wear them.
  • Moving on deck and the man overboard drill.
  • The liferaft, the grab bag and the EPIRB.
  • Flares: where they are and how to fire each type.
  • Fire extinguishers, the fire blanket and the gas system.
  • The VHF radio and how to send a distress call.
  • The first aid kit and any medical needs on board.
  • Bilge pumps, seacocks and the wooden bungs.
  • Seasickness, sun and how to use the heads (toilet).

Equipment varies by boat and by country, so always check the boat's own equipment and the charter company's list.

Lifejackets and buoyancy aids

Buoyancy is measured in newtons (N). Cruising yachts usually carry automatic inflating lifejackets of 150N.

TypeLifejacket, 150NBuoyancy aid, 50N
How it floatsInflates from a gas cylinderFoam, always buoyant
Turns a person face upYes, in most casesNo
Best useOn a yacht at seaDinghy sailing or watersports near help

Inflation. An automatic lifejacket inflates by itself within seconds of immersion. It also has a pull toggle to fire it by hand and a tube to top it up by mouth.

Fitting. Tighten the waist strap until it is snug and the lifejacket cannot be pulled over your head. Always fasten the crotch straps (between the legs), or it can ride up over your face in the water.

Checking. Check that the gas cylinder is screwed in tight and unused, and the automatic firing unit is in date. Many show a green indicator when ready.

When to wear one. Always at night, in rough weather or poor visibility, and in the dinghy. Non-swimmers and children wear one at all times on deck, children in a lifejacket made for their size. Otherwise, whenever the skipper asks.

Harnesses, tethers and jackstays

A lifejacket helps once you are in the water. A harness keeps you out of it. Most yacht lifejackets have one built in, with a ring at the chest for a tether (a short strap with a safety hook at each end).

  • Jackstays. These strong lines run along each side deck from bow to stern, so you can walk the length of the boat without unclipping.
  • Clip on first. At night and in rough weather, clip on to a strong point before you come up the companionway (the steps from the cabin), and stay clipped on.
  • Strong points only. Clip to jackstays and to fittings made for the purpose, never to the guardrails (the wires round the deck edge).
  • Short is better. A tether that keeps you on deck is safer than one long enough to let you over the side. Many have two lengths for this reason.

Liferaft and grab bag

The liferaft is packed in a canister on deck or a soft valise in a cockpit locker. Find out where it is. Its painter (the line that ties it to the yacht) also fires the gas that inflates it.

  1. Check that the painter is tied to a strong point on the yacht.
  2. Throw the liferaft over the leeward (downwind) side.
  3. Pull out the painter until it comes tight, then give a sharp tug to inflate the raft.
  4. Pull it alongside and board it as dry as you can, heaviest crew first.
  5. Once everyone is in, cut the painter with the knife kept by the raft entrance.
Watch out

A yacht, even a damaged one, is easier to find and safer than a liferaft. Only abandon her when she is sinking or on fire beyond control. The old rule: step up into the liferaft, never down.

Grab bag. This waterproof, floating bag is kept near the companionway, ready to take into the raft. It usually holds a handheld VHF, flares, a torch, drinking water, seasickness tablets, sun cream, essential medicines and copies of passports and the boat's papers.

EPIRB and PLB

These beacons send a distress signal by satellite to the rescue services, with an identity code and usually a GPS position.

  • EPIRB. The Emergency Position Indicating Radio Beacon belongs to the boat. Some float free automatically if the yacht sinks. Others are switched on by hand.
  • PLB. A Personal Locator Beacon is smaller and belongs to a person. You carry it on your lifejacket and switch it on yourself, holding the aerial upright.

Every beacon must be registered in the country it is coded for, so rescuers know whose it is and whom to call. Check the battery date, and use only the maker's self-test.

Flares

Flares show rescuers where you are. Each type has its own job:

FlareWhen to use it
Red handheld flareAt night or by day, when rescuers are close and need to pinpoint you
Orange smokeBy day, to show a helicopter or aircraft where you are and the wind direction
Red parachute rocketTo raise the alarm at a distance, when nobody is in sight

Firing safely. Read the instructions on each flare before you need it. Hold a handheld flare at arm's length on the downwind side, pointing away from you, and do not look at it. Fire a rocket upwards, angled slightly downwind, never when a helicopter is close.

Expiry. Every flare has an expiry date. Hand old flares in through an approved disposal scheme. Never throw them away or set them off.

Fire and gas

Extinguishers. Learn where each one is. Pull the pin, aim at the base of the flames and sweep from side to side. If a fire cannot be put out quickly, get everyone on deck and call for help.

Fire blanket. This hangs by the galley (kitchen). For a pan fire, turn off the gas, pull the tapes, lay the blanket over the pan and leave it in place. Never pour water on burning oil.

Gas safety. Bottled gas is heavier than air, so a leak sinks into the bilge (the lowest part of the hull) and can explode. Turn the gas off at the bottle when the stove is not in use. The gas locker drains overboard: keep its drain clear and do not store anything else there. If you smell gas, turn it off at the bottle, use no flames or electrical switches, open the hatches and pump the bilge with the manual pump.

Leaks, pumps and first aid

Bilge pumps. Most yachts have an electric bilge pump, often automatic, and a manual pump worked from the cockpit. Know where its handle is kept, and look in the bilge every day.

Seacocks. These valves close the through-hull fittings (holes in the hull) for the toilet, sinks and engine cooling water. Learn where each one is, and which the skipper wants closed when not in use.

Wooden bungs. A tapered softwood plug should be tied next to each seacock. If a fitting breaks, you hammer the bung into the hole to stop the water.

First aid kit. Know where it is and what is in it. Bring your own medicines, and tell the skipper about any condition that matters. For serious injury or illness, the coastguard can give medical advice by radio.

Practise it on the water

On the Introduction to Yachting course the skipper gives a full safety briefing on the first day, and you give one yourself before the week ends. You fit your own lifejacket and harness, find every seacock and bung, and handle the safety gear so you know where everything is before you need it.
