Two ways to moor stern-to

In most Mediterranean harbours you do not lie alongside a pontoon. You moor stern-to: the stern (back) of the boat is tied to the quay and the bow (front) is held off by a line or an anchor. It fits many boats into a small space and lets you step ashore over a passerelle (gangway).

There are two versions:

  • Laid moorings (lazy lines). Most marinas, and many town quays in Croatia and Greece, have heavy ground lines laid on the seabed. From each berth a thinner line, the lazy line, runs from the quay down to a mooring chain. You pick it up at the stern and take it to the bow.
  • Your own anchor. On many town quays in the Greek islands and parts of Turkey there are no lazy lines. You drop your anchor well out from the quay and reverse in, laying chain as you go.

The approach is the same in both cases. Only what holds the bow differs.

Prepare before you go in

Most mooring problems start with a crew that is not ready. Get everything set up well outside the harbour, in open water where you have time.

  • Fenders on both sides, at the widest part of the boat and near the stern. You may end up between two boats, and you will not always know which side gets the pressure.
  • Two stern lines, one each side, cleated on board, coiled and led outside everything so they can be thrown or handed ashore cleanly.
  • A boathook ready at the stern to pick up the lazy line.
  • Windlass (anchor winch) switched on and the anchor ready to drop, if you are using your own.
  • Roles briefed. Who handles each stern line, who takes the lazy line or runs the windlass, who fends off. Agree simple hand signals, because the helm cannot hear the bow over the engine.

If there is a marinero (marina attendant), call the marina on VHF or by phone before you arrive. They will usually point you to a berth and hand you the lazy line.

Read the wind, the gap and your prop walk

Before you commit, stop off the berth and look. Where is the wind coming from, and how strong is it? Is the gap wide enough for your beam plus fenders? Are the neighbours' lines or anchor chains in the way?

Then think about prop walk. A propeller turning in reverse pushes the stern sideways as well as backwards. On most charter yachts with a right-handed propeller, the stern kicks to port (left) in astern. Check this for your boat in open water: put the engine in reverse with the wheel centred and watch which way the stern swings.

Use it. If the stern kicks to port, approach so that kick helps your final line-up, or start your turn early so the walk does not push you onto the boat next door. With a crosswind, approach from the upwind side and aim slightly upwind of the gap, because the bow will blow downwind the moment you slow down.

The approach, step by step

Here is the main sequence for a laid mooring, with the extra steps for your own anchor marked.

  1. Line up some distance from the quay, stern towards the berth, and start going astern slowly. Get steerage (enough flow over the rudder to steer) before you reach the line of boats.
  2. Own anchor: drop the anchor about 3 to 4 boat lengths out from the quay, in line with your berth. Let chain out steadily as you reverse so it lies in a straight line on the seabed. Do not let it pile up, and do not brake it until you are nearly in.
  3. Reverse slowly, using short bursts of power and neutral. Approach no faster than you would be happy to hit the quay at.
  4. About a metre off, stop the boat with a touch of forward. The crew steps or passes the windward (upwind) stern line ashore first, then the other.
  5. Lazy line: pick it up with the boathook at the stern and hand it forward. Walk it along the side of the boat, outside the stanchions, to the bow.
  6. Pull the lazy line in hand over hand until it is tight, then cleat it at the bow.
  7. Own anchor: tension the chain with the windlass, gently, until it holds the bow and the anchor has bitten.
  8. Adjust the stern lines so the stern is far enough off the quay that it cannot touch if the boat surges, but close enough to step ashore on the passerelle. Engine in neutral, then off.

Keep everything slow. A boat going astern at walking pace is easy to stop. One going at three knots is not.

Watch out

A rope near the propeller can wrap it in seconds and stop the engine just when you need it. Keep stern lines out of the water. When you lift the lazy line, put the engine in neutral and walk it forward quickly so it sinks away from the stern. Never motor ahead hard while any line hangs over the stern.

Crossed anchors

When you use your own anchor, the most common problem is laying your chain across a neighbour's. When one of you leaves, the anchor comes up with the other boat's chain on it.

To avoid it, look at the angle of the neighbours' chains before you go in, drop your anchor straight out from your own berth rather than at an angle, and go in after boats either side of you have settled, if you can. If someone drops across your chain, tell them politely and early.

If you do lift someone else's chain, stop the windlass. Pass a line under their chain, make both ends fast on board, then lower your anchor a little so it drops free. Release one end of the line and let their chain fall back.

Leaving the berth

Brief the crew before you start. On a lazy line, rig the stern lines as slip lines (doubled back to the boat around the quay ring) so you can release them from on board. Let the lazy line go at the bow and wait until it has sunk well away. Then slip the stern lines and motor out gently ahead.

With your own anchor, slip the stern lines and bring in the chain with the windlass while the helm gives gentle ahead power to take the load off it. Keep the boat in line with the chain until the anchor is off the bottom.

Crosswind tips

  • Get the windward stern line ashore first. It stops the stern blowing downwind.
  • A little more speed gives more control. Do not crawl so slowly that the wind takes over the bow.
  • With your own anchor, drop a little upwind of your berth. The chain then holds the bow up as you reverse.
  • If it goes wrong, go out, reset and try again. There is no shame in a second approach.

Practise it on the water

On our courses you practise this in real harbours, with laid moorings and with your own anchor, until each crew member has done every role. You start in light wind and move on to crosswind berths once the routine feels calm.
