Where the wind comes from

Everything on a sailing yacht starts with the wind direction. Before you touch a sheet (a line that controls a sail), find where the wind is coming from. Feel it on your face and ears, look at the wind indicator at the top of the mast, and watch the ripples on the water.

A sail cannot drive a yacht straight into the wind. There is a sector, roughly 45° either side of the wind, where the sails simply flap and the boat slows down. This is the no-go zone. The exact angle varies with the boat and the sea: a modern cruising yacht in flat water may point a little higher, while a heavy boat in a short chop will not sail as close. If you turn into the no-go zone and stop, you are "in irons": stuck head to wind with no steering.

Every other direction is open to you. The names for the angles between your course and the wind are the points of sail.

The points of sail

Each point of sail describes the angle between the bow (the front of the boat) and the wind. The side the wind comes over matters too: with the wind on the starboard (right) side you are on starboard tack, and with it on the port (left) side you are on port tack. The names below apply on either tack.

Close-hauled

About 45° to the wind, just outside the no-go zone. Both sails are pulled in tight, close to the centreline of the boat. The yacht heels (leans over) most on this point, the motion is livelier and spray may come over the bow. You steer with small movements, keeping the sails just full.

Close reach

Around 60° to the wind. Ease (let out) the sheets a little from close-hauled. The boat heels less and usually speeds up.

Beam reach

The wind is at 90°, straight across the side of the boat. The sails are eased about halfway out. For most cruising yachts this is a fast and comfortable point of sail, and a good one to learn on.

Broad reach

Around 135°, with the wind coming from behind the beam, over the back quarter of the boat. The sails are well out. The boat sits more upright and the wind feels lighter than it really is. In a swell she may start to roll.

Running

The wind is from directly behind, at about 180°. The mainsail is eased right out, and the headsail (the sail at the front) often hangs limp in its shadow. You can set the headsail out on the opposite side to the mainsail, which is called goose-winging. Running is where an accidental gybe is most likely, so many skippers prefer a broad reach and change direction in steps.

Heading up and bearing away

Turning towards the wind is called heading up. Turning away from it is bearing away. Sail trim follows a simple rule: as you head up, sheet in; as you bear away, ease out. Each change of course needs a change of trim.

A reliable test is to ease the sheet until the luff (the front edge of the sail) starts to flutter, then pull it in just until the flutter stops. That is roughly the right setting for your course.

Two faults are common. Sheets pulled in too tight on a reach make the boat heel and slow down, even though the sail looks full and quiet. Sheets eased too far let the sail flap, which costs speed and wears the cloth. On a broad reach or a run, also check that the boom is not pressing against the shrouds (the wires that hold the mast up from the sides).

Tacking and gybing

To change from one tack to the other, you turn either the bow or the stern through the wind.

Tacking means turning the bow through the wind. You use it to make progress upwind, zigzagging from close-hauled on one tack to close-hauled on the other. The headsail sheet is released on the old side and pulled in on the new side. The boom moves only a short way, because the mainsail is already in tight. The helm (the person steering) calls "Ready about", the crew answers "Ready", and the helm calls "Tacking" or "Lee-oh" as they turn.

Gybing means turning the stern (the back of the boat) through the wind. You use it when sailing downwind. This time the wind gets behind the mainsail and swings the boom across the whole width of the boat. In a controlled gybe, the helm calls "Ready to gybe", the crew pulls the mainsheet in to bring the boom towards the centre, the helm turns, and the mainsheet is eased out again on the new side.

Watch out

An accidental gybe happens when you steer too far downwind and the wind catches the back of the mainsail. The boom then slams across with no warning. It can seriously injure anyone in its path and damage the rig. On a run, keep your head below the boom's path, keep the wind a little on the side opposite the boom, and rig a preventer (a line holding the boom forward) when the skipper asks for one.

True and apparent wind

The true wind is what you feel standing still on the quay. Once the boat moves, its own speed creates a breeze, and the two combine into the apparent wind: the wind you feel on deck and the wind the sails actually use. Sailing upwind, the apparent wind feels stronger and comes from further forward than the true wind. Sailing downwind, it feels lighter. This is why a gentle broad reach can turn into a hard beat when you head up. The wind indicator and the telltales always show the apparent wind.

Reading the telltales

Telltales are short strips of wool or ribbon on both sides of the headsail, a little back from the luff. They show how the air flows over the sail, and they react well before the sail itself starts to flutter.

  • Both streaming straight back: the sail is well trimmed. Keep going.
  • The windward one lifts or spins (on the side the wind comes from): the sail is too far out for your course. Sheet in, or bear away a little.
  • The leeward one lifts or spins (on the side away from the wind): the sail is in too tight. Ease the sheet, or head up a little.

Close-hauled, the sheet is already in tight, so you correct with the helm: bear away when the windward telltale lifts, head up when the leeward one does. On a reach, hold your course and trim the sheet. Many mainsails also have telltales on the leech (the back edge). When they stream back, the mainsail is not pulled in too hard.

Practise it on the water

On the Introduction to Yachting course you sail every point of sail, taking turns on the helm and on the sheets until trimming by the telltales becomes a habit. You practise tacking and controlled gybes many times in moderate wind before you do them in a stronger breeze.
