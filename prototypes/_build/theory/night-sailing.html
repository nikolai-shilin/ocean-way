Prepare before dark

Everything on a yacht is harder in the dark, so do the work while it is still light. Check your charter contract first, because many charter companies do not allow sailing at night. Then, an hour or so before sunset:

  • Reef early. If the forecast shows more wind overnight, put the reef in before dark. Shaking out a reef at night is easy, but putting one in on a dark, heeling deck is slow and risky.
  • Food and drink. Cook a hot meal before sunset, fill flasks with hot water and prepare snacks, so nobody has to cook below at night.
  • Jackstays and harnesses. Rig the jackstays (strong lines along each side deck). Each crew member adjusts their own lifejacket and harness in daylight, over warm clothes.
  • Torches and lights. Test the light on every lifejacket and on the lifebuoy. Each person on watch needs a small torch with a red light, and the boat needs one powerful searchlight. Keep spare batteries where you can find them.
  • Navigation lights. Switch them on and check them before sunset. The navigation lights guide in this series explains which lights to show under sail and under engine.
  • Deck and chart. Coil every line so you can find it by feel, and close the forward hatches. Mark the route, the dangers and the lights you expect to see on the chart, and switch the chartplotter to night mode.

Night vision

Your eyes take about 20 to 30 minutes to adapt fully to the dark. One look at a white torch or a bright phone screen undoes it in a second. That is why everything you use on deck at night should have a red light, and why you dim every screen as far as you can.

Come on deck a few minutes before your watch starts, so your eyes are ready when you take over. Below, use the red cabin lights if the boat has them.

Tip

To see a faint light or the outline of a boat, look slightly to one side of it rather than straight at it. The edge of your eye is more sensitive in the dark than the centre.

Keeping a lookout at night

At night you see lights, not boats. Scan the whole horizon slowly, one section at a time, every few minutes, including behind the sails and astern. The lights you see tell you what each vessel is and which way she is heading.

Distance is hard to judge at night. A bright light can look close when it is far away. These rules of thumb help:

  • A large ship's masthead lights can be seen from about 6 miles, and her sidelights from about 3 miles. Once you can see her red or green, she is getting close.
  • A ship at 18 knots covers 3 miles in 10 minutes, and a fast ferry at 30 knots in 6 minutes.
  • Bright shore lights, such as a town or a coast road, can hide a vessel in front of them.

Take a compass bearing on every light that comes closer. Use the hand-bearing compass, or line the light up with a stanchion (a guardrail post) while the helm holds a steady course. Repeat it every few minutes. If the bearing does not change while the light comes closer, there is a risk of collision. The right of way guide explains who gives way and how to act.

In the Saronic Gulf, ferries and fast catamarans run between Piraeus and the islands until late in the evening. Ships at anchor off Piraeus show bright deck lights that can hide their navigation lights. Small fishing boats work close to the coast at night, some with a very bright lamp, and their nets may be marked only by small flashing lights.

Watch systems for a small crew

A watch system shares the night, so that someone awake and rested is always on deck while the others sleep. Agree it before dark and write it in the logbook.

  • Crew of four. Two watches of two people, three hours on and three hours off.
  • Crew of six. Three watches of two people, two hours on and four hours off.
  • The skipper. Ideally the skipper stays outside the watch system and is called when needed.

When the watch changes, the new watch comes up ten minutes early. The old watch hands over the course, the sails and any traffic in sight before going below. Off watch, go to sleep even if you do not feel tired.

The skipper's night orders

Before going below, the skipper writes the night orders in the logbook: the course to steer, the sail plan, the dangers on the route and when to call the skipper.

A light with a steady bearing that comes closerCall the skipper at once
A light you cannot identifyCall the skipper
The wind rises or shifts, or the boat is overpoweredCall the skipper
Rain, mist or poor visibilityCall the skipper
Anyone on deck feels unwell, very cold or too tiredCall the skipper
Every hourWrite the position, course, speed and wind in the log

A good skipper would rather be called ten times for nothing than once too late. Tell the crew this in plain words.

Staying clipped on

A person who falls overboard at night is very hard to find, even with a lifejacket light. So the night rules apply to everyone on board, the skipper included:

  • Wear a lifejacket with a harness and a light whenever you are on deck after dark.
  • Clip your tether on before you come up the companionway (the steps from the cabin), and stay clipped on in the cockpit.
  • Go forward only when you must, clipped to the jackstay, and tell the cockpit before you go.
  • Use the heads (toilet) below, never the side of the boat.

If someone does fall in, press the MOB button on the chartplotter at once, then follow the man overboard drill.

Cold and tiredness

Even after a hot Greek summer day, it gets cold at sea after midnight, and dew soaks anyone sitting still in the cockpit. Wear more layers than you think you need, with a waterproof jacket and a hat on top.

Most people feel at their worst between about two and five in the morning. Reactions get slower, seasickness gets worse and simple sums go wrong. Keep going with hot drinks and easy food, and check each other's bearings and plotted positions. Try to sleep for a few hours in the afternoon before a night passage.

Navigating by lights

At night the coast is dark, but lighthouses tell you where you are. Each has its own light characteristic printed on the chart: the colour, the pattern of flashes and the period in seconds. The buoyage guide explains how to read them. To identify a light, time two or three full cycles with a stopwatch and compare them with the chart.

  • Range. The chart gives how far the light can be seen in clear weather. From a low cockpit, a light may only appear once it rises above the horizon.
  • Loom. On a clear night you may see the glow of a powerful light in the sky before the light itself comes into view.
  • Fixes. Bearings on two or three lighthouses give a position fix at night, as landmarks do by day.

Check the chartplotter against the lights you see. If a light is not where you expect it, find out why.

Entering harbour at night

Enter a harbour at night only if you know it from daylight, or if the pilot book shows a simple, well-lit entrance. At most Greek harbours, a light on the end of each breakwater marks the way in. Coming in from sea in Europe, which uses IALA region A buoyage, the red light is on your port side and the green light on your starboard side.

Inside, many dangers are unlit: yachts at anchor, mooring lines, buoys and small boats. Come in slowly, with a crew member at the bow pointing a torch ahead, never back at the helm.

Watch out

If you are unsure of a harbour in the dark, do not go in. Stay at sea until dawn, or anchor in a bay you know with plenty of room. A few hours of waiting are better than a damaged boat.
