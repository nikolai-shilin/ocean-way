Choosing the bay

A good night at anchor is decided before you lower anything. Start with the forecast, not with the view. Look at the wind expected overnight and tomorrow morning, then look for a bay that is open to anything else but closed to that direction.

  • Shelter. Land should lie between you and the forecast wind. Watch for winds that change through the night. In the Aegean, a meltemi (the strong summer northerly) can blow hard into a bay that looked calm at lunchtime, and katabatic gusts can drop off high ground at night.
  • Holding ground. Sand and mud hold well. Weed, including the seagrass meadows common in the Mediterranean, holds poorly because the anchor slides over it. Rock gives no reliable hold and can trap the anchor. In some areas anchoring on seagrass is restricted, so check local rules.
  • Depth. Somewhere between about 4 and 12 metres suits most charter yachts. Too shallow and you may touch at low water or when the boat swings. Too deep and you will run out of chain.
  • Swinging room. You need a clear circle around your anchor at least as large as your chain length plus the length of the boat.

The chart and a good pilot book will show the bottom type and depths. Your eyes and the depth sounder (echo sounder) confirm them.

Scope: how much chain

Scope is the length of chain you let out compared with the depth. For all-chain rode (the line between anchor and boat), use at least 4 to 5 times the depth in settled weather, and more if wind is forecast. Measure depth from the bow roller, so add a metre or so to the sounder reading. Tidal range in most of the Mediterranean is small, but where it is larger, use the depth at high water.

Charter yachts typically carry 50 to 80 metres of chain, and it varies, so find out what you have before you leave the base. Chain is usually marked every 10 metres with paint or coloured tags. Learn the code on your boat.

Longer scope matters because the anchor holds best when the pull is horizontal. The weight of the chain lying on the seabed keeps the pull low and absorbs snatching as the boat moves.

Lowering and setting the anchor

  1. Motor slowly into the bay and pick your spot, allowing for where the boat will lie once the chain is out.
  2. Approach head to wind (bow pointing into the wind) and stop the boat over the spot.
  3. Lower the anchor to the bottom with the windlass. Do not let it drop in free fall. A heap of chain on top of the anchor can foul it.
  4. As the boat drifts back or you go slowly astern, let out chain steadily so it lies in a line on the seabed rather than piling up.
  5. When the planned scope is out, hold the chain and let the boat come up against it. You will feel the bow swing to face the anchor.
  6. Set it under reverse power: build up gradually to a firm astern load and hold it for 20 to 30 seconds. If the boat keeps moving, the anchor is dragging. Lift it and try again.

Check that it is holding by taking a transit: two fixed objects ashore that line up, such as a rock in front of a building, roughly abeam (at right angles to the boat). If they stay in line under reverse, you are not moving. A compass bearing on a fixed point works too. Recheck it after a while and whenever the wind gets up.

Securing for the night

Once the anchor is set, rig a snubber: a length of stretchy rope hooked onto the chain and made fast on a bow cleat. Let out a little more chain so the snubber takes the load. It saves the windlass from strain and stops the chain jerking the boat. Catamarans use a bridle, a snubber with two arms led to each bow.

Set an anchor alarm. Most chart plotters and many phone apps let you mark the anchor position and sound an alarm if the boat moves outside a set radius. Set the radius to about your chain length plus the boat length, so normal swinging does not trigger it.

Look at the boats around you. Yachts on chain swing differently from boats on rope, and catamarans move differently from monohulls. If you anchor too close, you may meet at 3 a.m. after a wind shift. By custom, the boat that arrived first has the stronger claim to its space, so if you are the newcomer, it is up to you to leave room.

Tip

In clear Mediterranean water, the best check of all is to look. Put on a mask and snorkel and swim over the anchor. You will see whether it has dug in, is lying on its side, or has caught on a patch of weed. A few minutes in the water can save a sleepless night.

Shore lines in Turkey and Greece

In many small bays on the Lycian coast, around Göcek, and in parts of Greece, there is no room to swing. Instead you drop the anchor and reverse towards the shore, then take a line from the stern to a rock, tree or ring ashore. Some bays have bollards or rings fixed for this.

  • Drop the anchor well out, as for Mediterranean mooring, so there is plenty of chain when you stop.
  • One crew member takes the shore line ashore by dinghy or swims with it. Lead it round the tree or rock and back to the boat, so you can release it from on board.
  • Protect trees with a sleeve or a strop. Do not tie to anything that could pull loose.
  • Think about the wind direction. A shore line works best with the wind blowing off the land. If wind is forecast from the side, use two shore lines, or choose another bay.

Weighing anchor

To lift the anchor, one crew member works the windlass and signals to the helm. Motor gently towards the chain so the windlass is not dragging the boat. Point the direction of the chain with an arm. When the chain is straight up and down, the anchor should break out. Raise it slowly, rinse off mud before it comes aboard, and make it fast before you speed up.

Never drive over your own chain, and keep fingers and feet clear of the windlass while it runs.

Practise it on the water

On our courses you practise this in different bays every day, choosing the spot, working out the scope and setting the anchor under power. You also take shore lines in narrow coves and swim over the anchor to see how well it has set.
