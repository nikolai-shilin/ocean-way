One system, two regions

Buoys and beacons (fixed marks on posts or rocks) follow the IALA Maritime Buoyage System, agreed worldwide. Every mark gives you four clues: its colour, its shape, its topmark (the small shape on top) and, at night, its light.

The world is split into two regions, which differ only in the colours of the lateral marks that show the sides of a channel.

  • Region A. Europe, the Mediterranean including Greece and Turkey, Africa, most of Asia, Australia and New Zealand. Coming in from sea, red marks are left to port.
  • Region B. North, Central and South America, Japan, South Korea and the Philippines. The lateral colours are reversed, so coming in from sea, red marks are left to starboard.

All other marks are the same in both regions. Everything below describes Region A, where we sail.

Lateral marks: the sides of the channel

Lateral marks show the two edges of a channel, like the kerbs of a road.

  • Port-hand mark. Red and can-shaped (a flat-topped cylinder), with a red can as topmark if one is fitted. Any light is red.
  • Starboard-hand mark. Green and cone-shaped, with a green cone pointing up as topmark if one is fitted. Any light is green.

Lateral lights may use any rhythm except two flashes followed by one, Fl(2+1), which is kept for preferred-channel marks.

Direction of buoyage

Port and starboard here mean your sides when you travel in the direction of buoyage. Near a harbour, river or estuary, that means coming in from seaward. Along open coasts it is set by the authorities and generally runs clockwise around the continents. Where it could be unclear, the chart shows it with an arrow symbol. Leaving harbour, everything reverses: you keep red marks to starboard.

Preferred-channel marks

Where a channel divides, a modified lateral mark shows the main branch. Its light is Fl(2+1), in the colour of the mark's main body.

  • Preferred channel to starboard. A red can with one broad green band. Leave it to port and the main channel lies to starboard.
  • Preferred channel to port. A green cone with one broad red band. Leave it to starboard to stay in the main channel.

Cardinal marks: which side is safe

A cardinal mark stands to the north, east, south or west of a danger such as a rock, shoal or wreck. Its name tells you the safe side: pass north of a north cardinal, east of an east cardinal, and so on. All cardinals are black and yellow, with two black cones as topmark, and any light is white.

The topmark is the key to the colours. The black on the mark is where the cones point.

For the lights, think of a clock face. East is at three o'clock and gives three flashes. South is at six and gives six, then one long flash so it cannot be mistaken for three or nine. West is at nine and gives nine. North, at twelve, flashes without a break.

Watch out

A cardinal mark tells you which side is safe, not how far the danger reaches. A shoal can extend some way beyond the mark, so give it a good berth and check the chart.

Isolated danger and safe water

Isolated danger mark. Black with one or more broad red bands, and a topmark of two black balls, one above the other. It sits on or over a small danger, such as a single rock, with navigable water all around. Its light is white, a group of two flashes, Fl(2). Two balls, two flashes. Pass well clear on any side.

Safe water mark. Red and white vertical stripes, with a single red ball as topmark. It shows navigable water all around, often at the seaward end of a harbour approach or in mid-channel. Its light is white: isophase (Iso), occulting (Oc), one long flash every 10 seconds (LFl 10s) or Morse code A, Mo(A).

Special marks and wreck buoys

Special marks. Yellow, with a yellow X as topmark if one is fitted, and a yellow light if lit. They mark things such as swimming areas, cables and pipelines, outfalls, racing marks and fish farms. In summer, lines of small yellow buoys off Greek and Turkish beaches mark swimming zones. Keep out of them under power.

Emergency wreck-marking buoy. Blue and yellow vertical stripes, with a yellow upright cross (shaped like a plus sign) as topmark and a light alternating blue and yellow flashes. It marks a new wreck that is not yet on the chart, until the wreck is surveyed and permanently marked. Keep well clear.

Reading lights on the chart

Beside each lit mark, the chart prints a short code, the light characteristic. It gives the rhythm, the colour and the period: the time in seconds from the start of one sequence to the start of the next.

Colours are shown by letters: R red, G green, Y yellow, W white. No colour letter means white. A full entry such as Fl(2)R 6s 7m 4M reads: two red flashes every 6 seconds, from a light 7 metres high, visible for 4 nautical miles in clear weather. To time a light, watch two or three full cycles and count, or use a stopwatch.

Buoys in real life

A buoy is a clue to check, not a fact.

  • They move and go missing. A buoy can drag in a storm, break free or be removed for repair, and lights fail. Never steer from buoy to buoy without checking each one against the chart.
  • Many are unlit. A mark you passed easily by day may be invisible at night. Only marks with a light characteristic on the chart are lit.
  • Identify before you rely. Check colour, shape, topmark and light, then confirm your position by plotter, bearing or depth sounder.
  • Small harbours have few marks. In Greece and Turkey, many small harbours have no buoyed channel, just a light on each breakwater end: red to port and green to starboard as you enter. Isolated rocks are often marked by a beacon on the rock itself.

Enter an unfamiliar harbour in daylight when you can, and read the pilot book first.

Practise it on the water

On our courses you name every mark on the approach to a new harbour before you reach it: from the chart first, then by eye, and at night by counting and timing its light. Night passages along the Turkish coast and among the Greek islands give you real cardinal and harbour lights to identify from the cockpit.
