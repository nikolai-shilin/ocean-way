When to reef

Reefing means making a sail smaller when the wind gets up. The old saying is to reef when you first think of it. Reefing late in a rising wind is harder and wetter; shaking out an unneeded reef takes minutes.

  • Too much heel. The toe rail (the low edge round the deck) keeps dipping into the water. A cruising yacht sails faster more upright.
  • Heavy weather helm. Weather helm is the boat's tendency to turn up into the wind. If you need a lot of wheel to hold a straight course, or she rounds up in gusts, there is too much sail.
  • Gusts in the forecast. Plan for the gusts, not the average. Under high ground, around Göcek or off many Greek islands, gusts can be far stronger.
  • A breeze that builds. The afternoon sea breeze on the Turkish coast and the meltemi (the strong summer northerly) in the Aegean often strengthen after lunch. Reef in the shelter of the bay, before you head out.
  • Before night. If you will sail after dark, reef in daylight.

Reefing systems on charter yachts

Charter monohulls such as Bavaria, Dufour, Jeanneau and Beneteau yachts usually have one of two kinds of mainsail. Check which at the handover.

Slab reefing. The sail has reinforced eyes, called cringles, on the luff (front edge) and the leech (back edge) for each reef. You lower the sail part way and pull the cringles down to the boom, folding away the bottom strip. Most yachts have two or three reefs.

In-mast furling. The mainsail rolls up inside the mast, like a blind, so you can roll away as much as you need.

Slab reefing lines lead to the cockpit through clutches (rope jammers).

Putting in a slab reef, step by step

  1. Sail close-hauled or on a close reach with the headsail still drawing, so you can steer, and ease the mainsheet until the mainsail flaps.
  2. Ease the kicker (the vang, which holds the boom down) so the boom can rise.
  3. If there is a topping lift (a line from the masthead holding the boom up), take it up so the boom does not drop.
  4. Ease the main halyard to the mark for that reef, or until the luff cringle reaches the boom.
  5. Secure the luff with its reefing line or the hook. With single-line reefing, ease the halyard and take in the line a little at a time, watching both corners.
  6. Winch in the leech reefing line until the cringle is tight down onto the boom and slightly aft.
  7. Hoist the halyard until the creases along the luff disappear. Ease the topping lift, re-tension the kicker and sheet in.
  8. Tie any reef points (short ties along the reef row) round the folded sail only, never round the boom. With a stack pack (a sail bag on the boom), the fold simply drops into the bag.
Watch out

Never winch a reefing line against a halyard that is still tight: the sail or the line will give. If a reefing line suddenly becomes hard to turn, stop and look for the cause, such as the sail caught on the lazy jacks (lines that guide it onto the boom).

Reefing an in-mast furling main

Two lines do the work: the furling line rolls the sail into the mast, and the outhaul pulls it out along the boom.

  1. Sail on a close reach or a beam reach and ease the mainsheet until the sail is lightly loaded but not flogging.
  2. Set the boom at roughly a right angle to the mast, using the kicker.
  3. One person eases the outhaul slowly, keeping some tension on it.
  4. Another pulls in the furling line at the same rate.
  5. When enough is rolled away, lock the furling line, take up the outhaul to flatten the sail and trim.

Never force it. If the furling line goes stiff, stop at once, especially on an electric winch. A fold may be catching in the slot of the mast: let out a little sail and try again. A forced sail can jam in the mast. Details vary between makes, so follow the boat's handbook.

Furling the genoa

On charter yachts the genoa (a large headsail that overlaps the mast) rolls up round the forestay (the wire from the bow to the mast) on a roller furler.

  1. Ease the genoa sheet steadily, with turns kept on its winch, while a second person winches in the furling line.
  2. Keep a little tension on the sheet throughout, so the sail rolls tightly. A loose roll flaps and can unroll in a gust.
  3. Lock the furling line, sheet in again, and move the sheet car (the sliding block on the deck track) forward, so the sheet pulls down on the smaller sail as well as back.

Never winch hard on the furling line against a cleated sheet. When the genoa is fully rolled away, leave two or three turns of sheet round it, so the wind cannot unroll it.

Balancing the sails

A well-balanced boat needs only a little wheel to hold her course.

  • Too much mainsail. It turns the boat up into the wind, giving strong weather helm. Reefing the main is the cure.
  • Too little headsail. It has a similar effect. Roll away a lot of genoa under a full main and the weather helm gets worse.
  • Reduce in steps. A common order is a first reef in the main, then some genoa, then a second reef. The best order varies with the boat.
  • Use the traveller in gusts. Ease it to leeward, or ease the mainsheet briefly, to spill wind.

Aim for a light weather helm: let go of the wheel and the boat slowly turns into the wind.

Shaking out a reef

When the wind has eased for good, not just for a lull, shake out the reef by doing the job in reverse.

  1. Untie every reef point first. One forgotten tie can tear the sail.
  2. Head up, ease the mainsheet and the kicker, and take up the topping lift if fitted.
  3. Ease the reefing lines and free the luff cringle.
  4. Hoist the halyard until the luff is firm, then ease the topping lift, re-tension the kicker and sheet in.

With in-mast furling or the genoa, ease the furling line with turns on its winch while you pull out the outhaul or the sheet.

Tip

Be patient. The meltemi can drop for an hour and return as strong as before. If your evening anchorage is close, keep the reef in until you arrive.

Practise it on the water

On the International Bareboat Skipper course you reef and shake out at sea until you can brief a crew and do it without fuss. You use both slab reefing and in-mast furling, and learn to decide when to reef from the forecast and the feel of the boat.
