Man overboard: getting someone back

Prevention comes first, but every crew member should know the drill: what to do in the first seconds, when to call for help, how to return under engine or sail, and how to lift the person aboard.

In short
  • Clip on, wear a lifejacket and keep one hand for the boat.
  • Shout, throw the lifebuoy and danbuoy, point and press MOB.
  • Under engine, check for lines in the water and keep the propeller away from the person.
  • Under sail, use the quick-stop or reach-tack-reach, with a final approach on a close reach.

Staying on board

Getting someone back from the water is hard, even for a practised crew, so the best man overboard (also called person overboard) is the one that never happens.

  • One hand for yourself, one for the boat. When you move about the deck, keep hold of something solid: a grabrail, a shroud (a wire holding up the mast) or the boom when it is secured.
  • Lifejackets. Wear one when the skipper asks, and always at night, in rough weather and in the dinghy. It keeps your face clear of the water.
  • Clip on. At night and in rough weather, wear a harness and clip your tether to the jackstays (strong webbing lines that run along each side deck from bow to stern) before you leave the cockpit.
  • Move carefully. Stay low, use the windward (upwind) side deck, and never relieve yourself over the side. Use the heads (toilet) below.

The first seconds

When someone falls in, the boat sails away from them fast: at six knots, about three metres every second. The first moments decide how the recovery goes.

  1. Shout. Call "Man overboard" loudly, and say which side, so the whole crew and the helm know at once.
  2. Throw. Throw the horseshoe lifebuoy, the danbuoy (a floating pole with a flag and light that is easy to see in waves) and the lifesling if you carry one, as close to the person as you can. They give the person something to hold and mark the spot.
  3. Point. One crew member points at the person with an outstretched arm and does nothing else. A head in the water vanishes behind the first wave if nobody keeps it in sight.
  4. Mark the position. Press the MOB button on the chart plotter or GPS. It stores the position and shows the bearing and distance back to it.

Only then does the skipper choose how to get back, under engine or under sail.

When to call for help

A man overboard is grave and imminent danger to life. If the person is back aboard within a minute or two, you may not need outside help. But if you lose sight of them, cannot get back quickly, the water is cold or the crew is small, send a DSC distress alert and a Mayday at once. Rescuers can only help if they know.

The exact words are covered in our article on VHF radio procedure. Give one crew member this job, so the helm and the spotter can keep doing theirs. If the person is recovered after all, cancel the alert.

Recovery under engine

Motoring back is often the quickest and most controlled method. Before you start the engine, check that no lines are in the water: a sheet round the propeller leaves you with no engine at the worst moment.

  1. Furl or drop the headsail, and sheet the mainsail in tight or drop it.
  2. Start the engine and motor back towards the person.
  3. Make the final approach slowly from downwind, heading into the wind, so the boat stops easily.
  4. Aim to bring the person alongside the windward bow, well forward of the stern.
  5. Put the engine in neutral before you reach them, so the propeller has stopped turning, and keep it there while they are close to the boat.
Watch out

A turning propeller can cause terrible injuries. Never bring the stern towards a person in the water, and keep the engine in neutral whenever they are near it. If the person has to come aboard over the stern, stop the engine.

Under sail: quick-stop and heave-to

The quick-stop aims to stop the boat right away, as close to the person as possible.

  1. As soon as the alarm is raised, tack immediately without releasing the headsail sheet. This is sometimes called a crash tack.
  2. The headsail is now backed (held on the wrong side), and the boat heaves to: she lies almost stopped, drifting slowly sideways.
  3. From there the person is close by. The skipper can drift down towards them, sail a short loop back, or furl the headsail and recover under engine after checking for lines.

Under sail: reach, tack, reach

The reach-tack-reach method, sometimes called the figure of eight, brings the boat back under full control with sails that you can use as a brake.

  1. Put the boat on a beam reach (the wind across the side) and sail away for several boat lengths, enough room to manoeuvre.
  2. Tack, and sail back on a broad reach, aiming at a point a few boat lengths downwind of the person.
  3. From there, turn up onto a close reach and head straight for them for the final approach.
  4. On this course you can ease the sheets until the sails flap, which slows the boat. Pull them in a little to keep moving.
  5. Stop with the person on the leeward side, just forward of the shrouds, where the deck is lowest.

The test of a good approach is simple: if you can let the sails flap completely and still point at the person, you are on the right course. If the sails will not flap, you are not far enough downwind of the person. Bear away and go round again rather than arrive too fast.

Getting them back aboard

Reaching the person is only half the job. First attach them to the boat with a line, so they cannot drift away. Then decide how to lift them.

  • Freeboard. The height of the deck above the water is often more than a metre on a modern yacht. A tired, cold person in wet clothes cannot climb that on their own.
  • Lifesling. This floating sling on a long line goes under the person's arms. Once they are alongside, clip a spare halyard (a line used to hoist sails) to the sling and winch them up.
  • Bathing ladder. In calm water the stern ladder can be the easiest way up, but only with the engine stopped. In waves the stern pitches hard, so take great care.
  • Lifting with care. Someone who has been in the water for a while should be lifted as horizontally as possible, for example with a second line under the knees, and then kept lying down.

Cold shock and hypothermia

Sudden immersion causes cold shock: an involuntary gasp and fast breathing in the first minute or so. This is when people swallow water, and why a lifejacket that holds the face clear matters. Even in summer, Mediterranean water is cooler than the body, and after a long time in it a person becomes hypothermic (dangerously cold), weak and confused.

Once aboard, get them out of the wind, remove wet clothes and wrap them in dry layers or a sleeping bag. Give warm, sweet drinks only if they are fully conscious. Warm them gradually, keep them lying down and watch them closely. If they were unconscious, breathed in water or stay very cold, get medical advice by radio.

Practise it on the water

On the Introduction to Yachting course we drop a fender and bucket over the side without warning, and the crew runs the whole drill: shout, throw, point, press MOB. Everyone takes turns as spotter and at the helm, recovering under engine, with a quick-stop and with reach-tack-reach, until each of you can stop the boat beside the fender.
