Journeys · The Cyclades

Seven days
under sail

A slow week through the Greek islands on a catamaran: quiet bays, long lunches, and the kind of light that makes everyone look like they've been on holiday for a month.

Words by Elena Marsh · Photographs via Unsplash · 12 min read

Morning anchorage, first day out.

The first thing you notice is the quiet. Not silence, exactly: there's the slap of water against the hulls, a halyard knocking somewhere on the mast, a gull arguing with another gull. But after the airport, the ferry terminal, and the taxi with the radio turned all the way up, the island of Paros at seven in the morning feels like someone has finally turned the volume down.

We came for a week, eight of us on a forty-two-foot catamaran, with a skipper named Nikos who has been sailing these waters since he was tall enough to see over the wheel. The plan was loose by design. Sail three or four hours a day, anchor somewhere sheltered, swim, eat, repeat. Nikos would decide the route each morning based on the wind, which in the Cyclades in July means the meltemi, the strong northerly that can turn a pleasant crossing into a lively one.

By the second day, nobody was checking their phone. By the third, nobody knew what day it was. By the fourth, a debate broke out over whether we should simply never go home, which was settled only when someone pointed out that the boat had to go back eventually, and so, regrettably, did we.

ii.

Life on board

A catamaran is, in the most practical sense, a floating apartment with two front porches. The cabins are compact but private, each with its own small bathroom, and the deck is wide enough for eight people to sprawl without anyone touching. The net at the front, the trampoline, became the most fought-over spot on the boat, especially at sunset.

Mornings started early. Someone would put the coffee on, someone else would jump straight off the back step into water so clear you could count the stones on the bottom, and Nikos would study the sky and announce where we were going. Lunch was usually at anchor: tomatoes that tasted like tomatoes, feta, bread from whatever bakery we'd found the night before, and a bottle of something cold and local.

The days that stood out

Folegandros was the surprise. Smaller and steeper than its famous neighbour, it has a clifftop village that seems to hang over the sea and almost no traffic. We walked up at dusk, ate grilled octopus in a square lit with strings of bulbs, and walked back down in the dark, following the sound of the water to the harbour.

iii.

Two ways to go

The trip comes in two versions. The classic week is what we did: a skippered catamaran where the group shares the shopping and the cooking. The premium week swaps in a larger boat and a private chef, which turns every lunch into a small event and frees up everyone's evenings entirely.

Before you go

Pack less than you think. Soft bags only, because hard suitcases don't fit in the cabins. Bring a hat you don't mind losing to the wind, reef-safe sunscreen, and one nice outfit for the evening in Oia. Everything else, including the best version of yourself, tends to turn up on its own by day three.
